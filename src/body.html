Pair masses

Every pair of oppositely charged pions or kaons from one collision, by their combined mass √(E² − p²). Unrelated pairs make a smooth background; pairs from one resonance pile up at its mass: the ρ (775 MeV), K* (892), φ (1019), K⁰s (498). Subtracting like-sign pairs removes most of the background, as experiments do.

Energy ledger

Charge

Charge is never made or lost. A positron reaching a wall annihilates with one of its electrons, leaving it +1 e; a charged particle stopped in a wall leaves its charge there. “All together” changes only when you add or remove charge, or with beams.

What is simulated

Protons, neutrons, electrons, positrons, bare nuclei and atoms move in a two-dimensional chamberthree-dimensional box (the 3-D lab) in real units: ångströms, femtoseconds, electron-volts. An atom is a nucleus with bound electrons, whose count sets its charge, size, valence and spectrum. Free electrons are particles of their own, stepped many times per atom step; the step adapts to the fastest motion.

In the 3-D lab

The same physics in a cube: molecules take their real shapes (VSEPR angles), bonds resist twisting, and every collision, decay and reaction is worked out in three dimensions. Van der Waals wells stay doubled as in the flat game. Not in 3-D yet: placed objects and chips.

Forces

  • Coulomb's law between all charges, summed exactly at long range. Inside about one Bohr radius it is softened, as in the Kelbg/Deutsch potentials of plasma physics.
  • Van der Waals attraction and Pauli repulsion between atoms (Lennard-Jones with Universal Force Field parameters).
  • Hard cores. Closer than contact, atoms repel by the screened nuclear (ZBL) potential, so they can’t pass through each other. Atoms of one molecule meet a softer wall, as a flat page crowds a molecule’s groups.
  • Born repulsion between ions, sized by Shannon ionic radii, and stiff enough to balance any pair of opposite charges near contact.
  • Directional forces (Settings → Experimental): lone pairs on O and N (3-D) so hydrogen bonds point, σ-holes on Cl, Br and I (halogen bonds), and π clouds on aromatic rings (3-D) so rings stack offset.
  • Covalent bonds as Morse springs with tabulated bond energies and lengths, plus VSEPR bond angles (104.5° in water, 107° in ammonia).
  • Partial charges from electronegativity, which make hydrogen bonds. Formal charges spread as resonance spreads them (a carboxylate’s two oxygens share theirs). Bonds that can be drawn several ways take the average (Pauling’s order: 1.5 round benzene, 1.40 Å), and the resonance energy (150 kJ/mol for benzene) keeps the ring whole.
  • Metallic bonding calibrated so each metal melts near its real melting point. Settings → Experimental → Metals → Many-body makes it many-body: bonding grows as the square root of the neighbours (fitted to each metal’s cohesive energy, spacing, stiffness and vacancy energy), with fitted oxides, hydrides and alloys, and directional bonding so W, Mo, Fe and the other body-centred metals crystallize bcc.
  • Lorentz force on moving charges: free electrons move on exact arcs, so cyclotron orbits and E×B drift are exact at any field. Field gradients pull ferromagnets and paramagnets and push diamagnets, with the energy counted.
  • Electroweak. The photon, W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰ carry one force; the W and Z are heavy (80.4 and 91.2 GeV) through the Higgs field, which is why the weak force is weak. They are made in collisions above 80–90 GeV. The Higgs boson (125.2 GeV) is made above 125 GeV (gluon fusion at the LHC, e⁺e⁻ → ZH above 216 GeV) and decays at once: b b̄ 58%, WW* 21%, gg 8%, ττ 6%, γγ 0.23%.

Chemistry rules

  • Valence from the electron configuration (Aufbau with the Cr/Cu-type exceptions), the octet and duet rules, hypervalence for period-3+ atoms bonded to F, O or Cl.
  • Two radicals bond only if a third body can carry away the bond energy.
  • Atom-transfer reactions need collision energy above an Evans–Polanyi activation barrier; transition metals act as catalysts by lowering it.
  • Electrons hop between atoms when the Born–Haber balance (ionization energy, electron affinity, electrostatics) says it releases energy: that is ionic bonding. Bare protons attach to lone pairs (H₃O⁺, NH₄⁺).
  • Light breaks bonds it has the energy for (photodissociation); infrared light heats polar molecules.
  • Molecules are named by structure, which atoms are joined to which, so isomers such as ethanol and dimethyl ether, or butane and isobutane, each get their own name.

Atomic physics

  • Measured ionization energies and electron affinities for the outer electrons; Dirac's formula for the last one (uranium's is 132 keV deep); Slater's rules between, scaled so all of an atom's electrons together are bound by the measured total (760 keV for uranium).
  • Each ion's Coulomb pull on a free electron is softened over a core sized so the classical well is exactly as deep as the real level the electron would be captured into. A classical electron can then never be more tightly bound than quantum mechanics allows.
  • Electron capture by ions releases the binding energy as light: hydrogen cascades down its levels emitting Lyman, Balmer (656, 486, 434, 410 nm) and Paschen lines.
  • Orbitals (the Orbitals view, O, and Inspect): each occupied subshell is a hydrogen-like wavefunction screened by Slater’s rules, filled by Hund’s rule. s, p, d and f are drawn blue, amber, green and pink.
  • Quantum solver (Inspect, on request): a self-consistent Hartree–Fock–Slater calculation of the selected atom or ion (Herman–Skillman). It gives every orbital’s energy and size; the outermost comes within 1 eV of the measured ionization energy on average. Once run, the Orbitals view draws from it.
  • Ions shed electrons they cannot hold (anything beyond a stable anion; doubly charged atomic anions do not exist in empty space). The electron tunnels out through the ion's own repulsion, carrying the energy its departure releases, so the books still balance.
  • Ionization by electron impact, by hot collisions, by strong fields and by photons (photoelectric effect). Hot atoms glow with their own emission lines (flame tests) and with blackbody colour.
  • Gamma rays Compton-scatter, make electron–positron pairs above 1.022 MeV, and positrons annihilate into two 511 keV photons.

Placed objects (Objects tool, D)

  • Optics. Mirrors reflect; beam splitters send each photon one way or the other; a thin lens bends a ray at height h by h/f; an F2 flint prism refracts by Snell’s law (violet more than red). Glass absorbs below 300 nm; X-rays and γ rays pass through.
  • Detector screen and beam stop. Both stop light and fast particles (their energy goes into them, out of the chamber's books); antimatter annihilates there. The screen counts what hits it and draws its energy spectrum along itself.
  • Walls. Atoms meet every object as a soft wall on their edge, as at the chamber's walls (the energy is kept in the books); electrons bounce off. A wall object turns fast particles back too.
  • Foil. Fast charged particles crossing a few nm of metal are turned by each nucleus they pass (Rutherford). Most go nearly straight; a few bounce back, as Geiger and Marsden found in 1909.
  • Charged plates. A uniform field V/gap between two plates; outside them, none. Like the Lab's electric field, the energy it gives comes from the supply.

Nuclear physics

  • Binding energies from the Atomic Mass Evaluation (to zinc, and the heavy decay chains); elsewhere the semi-empirical mass formula. A uranium-235 fission gives about 170 MeV to its fragments and 8 MeV in prompt γ rays, as measured.
  • Real half-lives and decay modes for tabulated nuclides; otherwise Viola–Seaborg (α), Fermi-integral (β) and fissility (spontaneous fission) estimates. β particles get their real spectrum up to the end-point energy (for β⁺, two electron masses below the atomic Q-value).
  • A decaying nucleus keeps its electrons; an α daughter sheds its two extra at once. Each decay’s caption shows it close up, and Inspect charts a nucleus’s whole decay chain.
  • Fusion by tunnelling: the Gamow factor times each reaction’s S-factor. Above 150 keV D–T follows its measured cross section. Heavy ions above their Coulomb barrier touch and react (compound nucleus or quasi-fission).
  • Neutron capture, fission, (n,p) and (n,α) by cross-section ratios; fission fragments follow the double-humped yield curve with 2–3 neutrons. Above 20 MeV a neutron hit is a nuclear collision like any fast nucleus’s (spallation, fission).
  • Antimatter follows the same rules with charges reversed (CPT): antihydrogen has hydrogen’s spectrum and antiatoms bond like atoms. Electron and positron annihilate into two 511 keV γ rays, nucleon and antinucleon into about five pions (1.88 GeV), the energy exactly from the rest masses.

Accelerators

  • Ring collider (synchrotron). Eight 45° bending magnets with alternating focusing and defocusing quadrupoles between them (strong focusing: about 2.2 betatron oscillations a turn); straights hold the RF cavities, separators, kickers and detectors. All magnets follow the beams’ momentum as they speed up, B = p/(qρ).
  • Injection. A small linac brings each train to the injection energy and a kicker turns it onto the orbit. Electrons and positrons go in at 1.5 MeV, above transition; other beams at a twentieth of their final energy.
  • RF cavities and bunches. The cavities oscillate four times a turn, locked to the beam. The synchronous particle meets them at the phase φs that gives the energy the ramp calls for; late particles gain more and catch up, early ones less, so the train gathers into a bunch swinging about it (synchrotron oscillations) inside the RF bucket. Above transition (γ ≈ 2.1) φs moves to the falling side. 12% more energy a turn.
  • One ring or two. A particle and its antiparticle share one ring going opposite ways (as at LEP and the Tevatron), kept apart by separators while they speed up and brought together at full energy. Any other pair gets a ring each sharing the detector straight (as at the LHC and RHIC), at the same speed so their bunches meet every turn. Beams can be given different energies (as at the B factories), but then their bunches meet only when their timings line up.
  • Keeping the beams apart. By default the beams collide only at full energy; they can be kept apart or brought together at once. Brought together early they pull on each other harder (the kick falls as 1/(p·v)) and can blow each other apart: the beam–beam limit.
  • Beam–beam forces. Each particle is kicked by the other bunch’s charge, Δp = 2k·q₁q₂(1 + β₁β₂)/(b·v): opposite charges pinch, like charges push apart. Each beam particle stands for many real ones, giving a real collider’s beam–beam strength.
  • Synchrotron radiation. A bent charge radiates P = (2/3)ke²cγ⁴β⁴/ρ²: on a 2.4 nm radius an electron loses about 2.5 eV × γ⁴ a turn, so electrons are sped up only to 14.6 MeV. Protons radiate 10¹³ times less.
  • Real-world scale. The panel gives what these beams would need in real hardware: here the bending radius is 2.4 nm, so the magnets need 10⁵ T and more and the RF runs at petahertz.
  • Linear collider. Each linac pushes its beam through a set voltage, and a quadrupole magnet just before the collision point focuses it. (A real quadrupole focuses in one direction across the beam and defocuses in the other; a flat world has only the first.)
  • What collides: beam particles passing within 0.9 Å, mapped onto real impact parameters out to where Coulomb scattering turns them by 4°. e⁺e⁻ annihilate; matter and antimatter make mesons; nuclei touching above their barrier react, light ones tunnel and fuse below it; otherwise they Rutherford-scatter. Relativistic kinematics throughout; up to 10 GeV a particle in a ring.
  • Unstable particles as beams: muons, τ, charged pions and kaons, Σ±, Ξ⁻, Ω⁻, D⁺, D⁺s, B⁺ and their antiparticles go through the same magnets and cavities, radiate (a muon 207⁴× less than an electron) and decay on dilated clocks (a 1 GeV muon goes round about 10¹¹ times). They annihilate or collide as their kinds do.
  • New particles. e⁺e⁻ annihilate into whatever √s allows, by cross section: γγ, muon pairs above 211 MeV, pions, the φ at 1.02 GeV, hadrons above 1.1 GeV, τ pairs above 3.55 GeV. At 91.2 GeV nearly every collision makes a Z⁰ (two jets 70%, a lepton pair 3.4% each, neutrinos 20%); W⁺W⁻ above 161 GeV, ZZ above 182 GeV, ZH above 216 GeV. Electron rings stop at 14.6 MeV, so these need the linear collider. Hadron collisions make W and Z at their measured, tiny rates.
  • Unstable particles (muons, pions, kaons, η, Λ, τ …) fly at their true speeds, curve in fields (neutral ones leave dotted tracks), ionize what they pass, and decay in flight on dilated clocks: a π⁰ (25 nm) decays in the chamber, the charged ones leave unless decay is sped up (Lab › Time). Hadrons meeting nuclei scatter, are absorbed, make pions, swap strangeness (K̄N → Λπ) or annihilate, leaving the nucleus to boil off nucleons; stopped π⁻, Σ⁻, Ξ⁻, Ω⁻ are caught by atoms and absorbed, and a slow Λ may stay as a hypernucleus. Fast electrons give bremsstrahlung, γ rays above 8 MeV knock nucleons out, and a stopped μ⁺ often makes muonium.
  • New elements. Nuclei touching above their barrier react by energy per nucleon: light systems break up statistically; heavier ones fuse into a hot compound nucleus that boils off neutrons, protons and α or fissions, so ¹²C + ¹²C makes ²⁰Ne and ²³Na. Above Z₁Z₂ ≈ 1,700 most part again first (quasi-fission), which is why superheavy elements are so rare. Glancing passes trade nucleons; above 40 MeV a nucleon, fragmentation and spallation. The new nuclei then decay.
  • Exact books. Every collision shares its energy and momentum among its products by relativistic phase space, so both are kept exactly; rest mass made or destroyed is entered in the nuclear ledger and photons and neutrinos take what is left to the last eV.

Honest approximations

  • Two dimensions. Densities, neighbour counts and Maxwell–Boltzmann are 2-D, and van der Waals wells are doubled for the missing neighbours, so melting and boiling points are only rough. Molecules are flat (a tetrahedral carbon becomes a cross). The 3-D lab (main menu → 3-D) puts everything in a cube with real angles; placed objects and chips stay flat.
  • Ions in water pull twice as hard (flat game only). A flat ion has half the water around it and less screening, so with plain Coulomb salt would never dissolve; doubling the ion–water pull gives real shell strengths, and crystals dissolve from their corners. Side effect: partly covalent salts (AgCl) dissolve here like table salt. In 3-D the pull is the plain one.
  • Bound electrons are bookkept, not solved as waves (shells, configurations, energies). The Orbitals view draws from Slater’s rules, or from the quantum solver once run (relativity averaged: gold’s 6s 8.3 eV deep, measured 9.2); both are pictures and steer nothing. Molecular orbitals are computed only with Settings → Experimental → Bond orders → Quantum (GFN-xTB, up to 40 atoms). Free electrons are classical points unless Electrons as waves is on.
  • Rule-based chemistry. A bond's energy depends on the element pair and bond order, not the rest of the molecule (every O–H bond is the same), so some reaction heats are off by tens of kJ/mol.
  • Enlarged nuclei. A nucleus is hit where it is drawn (0.045 Å per nucleon, at least 0.25 Å) instead of femtometres, so reactions happen in a nanometre box; a pass is mapped onto real impact parameters, keeping relative rates true. Ultra-realistic mode uses true sizes.
  • Collisions shown simply. Above a few GeV, products come from strings breaking into hadrons along the collision axis (e⁺e⁻: two jets); below, they share phase space. Electrons don’t excite nuclei. Multiple scattering spreads several times wider in the flat chamber than through a real foil.
  • Tiny objects, flattened. The library’s chips, proteins, DNA, membranes and crystals lie flat (silicon’s bonds at right angles, DNA unwound into a ladder). Chips’ dopants are far denser than real, and a gate’s wells are parted by one seam of oxygen.
  • Sped-up decay. Motion and decay share one clock, about a picosecond per second of watching, so at real rates almost nothing decays: decay can be sped up (Lab › Time), or slowed down to a millionth so short-lived particles fly farther; resonances can be held until they have flown a few ångströms. Both are shown while on.
  • Huge fields. Bending thermal ions within nanometres takes about 10⁴ tesla; laboratory magnets reach about 45 T.
  • Shrunken accelerators. Rings 11 nm across need 10⁵ T magnets and petahertz RF (real: 8 T over kilometres, hundreds of MHz), and beams gain 12% a turn. Bunches are a few particles with no space charge, standing for many in the beam–beam forces. Beams collide within 0.9 Å, so collisions are far more frequent than real.
  • Energy is conserved. Every event balances kinetic, potential and electronic energy exactly, mass changes included. Insulated, the total stays constant to integration error; light leaving the box is counted in the ledger.

Shorter longest step

Longest time step

Calm matter is moved on in steps of 0.4 fs (busy scenes already take shorter ones). Shorter steps keep the energy books a little more exact.

In the flat game liquid water drifts 0.07 eV/ps at 0.2 fs and 0.06 at 0.1 fs, against 0.17; salt water −0.44 and −0.22 against −0.56. Costs ×1.6–1.8 and ×2.6–2.9 where the steps are at their longest; nothing in hot plasmas and ionized water, whose steps are shorter anyway.

In the 3-D lab a step costs about the same whatever its length, so the cost follows the number of steps: ×2 at 0.2 fs and ×3.3–4.1 at 0.1 fs (liquid water, salt water; measured in 3-D, 1.25), nothing where the steps are shorter anyway.

Resonance in large ring systems

Bonds that can be drawn more than one way take the average (1.5 round benzene), counted structure by structure up to 40 atoms. With this on, large flat ring systems (graphene flakes, up to 1,000 ring atoms) get exact orders from a determinant, and their resonance energy grows with them. Free while bonds stay put; a bonding change costs 12 ms at 200 ring atoms, 0.2 s at 1,000.

Detailed nuclear collisions

A nucleon (40 MeV–10 GeV) hitting a nucleus heavier than oxygen is followed through it collision by collision (an intranuclear cascade); what stays heats the nucleus, which boils off particles or splits. 1 GeV p on lead: 14.9 neutrons a reaction (measured about 17; statistical model 13.9), fission 21% (measured about 10%). About 3 ms a hit instead of 1.

The flat game only: in the 3-D lab nucleons do not strike nuclei yet (nuclear reactions between atoms are in the flat game only), so it changes nothing there. It stays set for the flat game.

Longer force reach

Force reach

Van der Waals and partial-charge forces stop at 6 Å (net charges reach farther while there are few of them). A longer reach keeps more of the pull between molecules. It runs on the WebAssembly force kernels, as 6 Å does (since 1.28). The potential energy changes when the reach does: the energy books step once.

Measured in the flat game on the same arrangement, liquid water binds 0.9 kJ/mol a molecule more at 9 Å and 1.6 at 12 Å (of about 41 kJ/mol to boil it off), brine 8.5 and 11. Costs ×1.3–2.6 at 9 Å and ×1.6–5.6 at 12 Å (brine, liquid water, 4,000 waters), more in big boxes.

In space the partners within reach grow as the cube of the reach (in the flat game as its square), so it costs far more in the 3-D lab: ×3.5–5 at 9 Å and ×7–17 at 12 Å (salt water, liquid water; measured in 3-D, 1.25).

Charges that respond to their surroundings

Induced charges

Partial charges follow the electric field from other molecules, ions and applied fields (charge equilibration), so a water molecule stays neutral but turns its dipole toward an ion. The energy is booked exactly; the lag between updates warms an insulated box a little. Molecules over 64 atoms keep fixed charges.

Fitted to water's measured polarizability (1.45 Å³, averaged in the plane); in liquid water the molecules' dipole rises from 2.19 to 2.46 D, and next to a sodium ion much more. Every 10th step costs ×1.4–1.8 and every step ×2.7–4.6 (brine, liquid water, 4,000 waters).

In 3-D the charges follow the field in all three directions; in liquid water they average 0.09 e (flat: 0.06). Cost: every 10th step ×1.4–2.4, every step ×2.5–3.2.

Polarizability

With Induced charges on (since 1.27). Normally the induced charges flow along the bonds, so a molecule polarizes only along its bonds: N₂, CO₂ and benzene not at all across them (out of benzene's plane nothing), and benzene's ring about 14 times too much. By direction gives each atom an induced dipole instead, which follows the field from other molecules, ions and an applied field with the polarizability its bonds have along and across them (bond polarizabilities fitted to 16 measured molecules), so every molecule polarizes in each direction as much as the real one: N₂ 2.20 / 1.51 Å³ along / across, CO₂ 3.96 / 1.96, benzene 12.2 in its plane and 6.6 out of it, water 1.47, methane 2.60. The dipoles are worked out together (each one feels the others'), softened at close range as Thole's model does so they cannot run away, and the energy books stay exact. Molecules of 2–64 atoms; a lone atom or ion is not polarized.

In the flat game, in the plane. In liquid water the molecules' dipole comes out 2.5–2.6 D (charges alone 2.17, induced charges 2.4). Costs ×1.8–2.9 on top of the induced charges (liquid water, brine; measured 1.27).

In liquid water the molecules' dipole comes out 2.6–2.7 D (charges alone 2.2, induced charges 2.4–2.5; measured in the liquid about 2.6–3.0 D). Costs ×1.2–4.2 on top of the induced charges (liquid water, brine; measured in 3-D, 1.27).

Quantum nuclei

Quantum nuclei
Bead forces

Atoms are not points: light nuclei spread and tunnel by their zero-point motion. With this on, each atom is a ring of beads (path-integral molecular dynamics), kept at the bath’s temperature. Free electrons, bare nuclei and fast particles stay points. Stiff bonds and bends are worked out on every bead, the smooth pairs once at each ring’s centre (ring-polymer contraction).

Zoomed in, the beads show as dots. At 300 K the H₂ bond spreads to 0.089 Å and lengthens from 0.746 to 0.764 Å (measured 0.766); heavy hydrogen spreads less. Insulated boxes need shorter steps (0.2 fs). Cost on liquid water: ×1.4 (8 beads), ×3 (16), ×6 (32).

In 3-D the rings spread in all three directions (a hydrogen’s ring in water: 0.16 Å across). Cost on liquid water: ×1.6 (8 beads), ×2.7 (16), ×6 (32).

Continuous bond orders

Bond orders

Normally bonds have whole-number orders and form or break as events. With this on, neutral molecules of non-metals (H, B, C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Br, I, Ge, As, Se, Sn, Sb, Te) keep no stored bonds: every pair’s bond order comes from the atoms’ positions each step, so bonds stretch and break smoothly and reactions find their own barriers (H + H₂: 0.41 eV, measured 0.42). Partial charges follow the orders, so water still hydrogen-bonds, and a charge can follow a proton from H₃O⁺ to the next water. Molecules touching a metal or ion, or with a doubly charged atom, keep stored bonds. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath. Steps are 2.6× shorter.

In the flat game the angles hold each bond's gap to its neighbours round the atom. Costs ×8 (liquid water) to ×15 (the molecule gallery), on the WebAssembly pass.

In 3-D the angles are VSEPR’s, double and aromatic bonds resist twisting, and single bonds twist against their measured barriers (ethane 0.126 eV). Runs in WebAssembly: ×7–14.

Quantum (GFN1-xTB): energy and forces come from the electrons, worked out every step by a tight-binding quantum chemistry (Grimme et al., 2017) with D3 dispersion, matching the reference program to 10⁻¹¹ eV. Bonds, charges and barriers all come from the electrons, so a proton hops between waters in a picosecond, which force fields cannot do. For small systems: up to 40 atoms (any element to radon), 160 orbitals; the rest of the world meets them through its usual forces. Bonds are drawn from the electrons (Mayer orders).

In the flat game the atoms stay in the plane; their orbitals are three-dimensional.

In the 3-D lab the atoms move in all three directions, as GFN1-xTB has them (hexazine's flat ring puckers there); a hydronium and five waters take 5.7 ms a step (the flat game 7.7).

Quantum method

Quantum method (since 1.26): GFN1-xTB, as since 1.22, or GFN2-xTB (Bannwarth, Ehlert and Grimme, 2019), its successor: the same kind of calculation with one orbital on hydrogen, the electrons' pull worked out to atomic dipoles and quadrupoles, and the D4 dispersion, whose strength follows each atom's charge. It matches the reference program (tblite) to 10⁻¹¹ eV and its forces to 10⁻¹⁰ eV/Å; in the game the forces are the energy's slopes to 10⁻⁶ eV/Å and an insulated hydronium with five waters keeps its energy to 10⁻⁴ eV over 200 steps, at about the same cost as GFN1. Measured against reference values (1.26): bond lengths alike (0.011 and 0.012 Å from the measured on 24 bonds); water's dimer alike (0.21 and 0.22 eV, measured 0.22); GFN2 closer for breaking molecules into atoms (both far too strong: 4.6 eV off on average, GFN1 6.6), but further off for barriers: H + H₂ (measured 0.42 eV) GFN1 0.15, while GFN2 binds H₃ by 0.40 eV; HCN to HNC (2.09 eV) GFN1 2.52, GFN2 3.18; a proton between two waters 2.7 Å apart (0.10 eV by g-xTB) GFN1 0.08, GFN2 0.04; and hexazine, which really falls apart into three N₂ releasing several eV: GFN1 holds it with a 0.54 eV barrier and releases 1.7 eV, GFN2 puts the ring 0.8 eV (flat) to 1.7 eV (puckered) below three N₂. So GFN1 stays the normal choice.

Spins

Spins (since 1.26): with Polarized, the electrons of each spin get orbitals of their own, as in the published spin-polarized GFN-xTB (Neugebauer, Bädorf, Ehlert, Hansen and Grimme, 2023), ported from the reference program (matched to 10⁻¹¹ eV and its forces to 10⁻⁶ eV/Å on 56 radicals, atoms and stretched bonds). Unpaired electrons line up, so a free atom or a radical gets the energy of its spin (nitrogen 3.1 eV, oxygen 1.5, hydrogen 1.0), oxygen's O₂ is the triplet it really is, and a bond pulled apart ends as two radicals instead of an unsettled half-and-half (H₂ given 8 eV: every step settles, against unsettled steps with eV jumps when paired). Each cluster finds its own spins (one Fermi level for both): the electrons are checked at every step for a lower spin arrangement, and when one appears the switch's energy goes to the heat bath. Measured against reference values (1.26): breaking 23 molecules into atoms 2.7 eV off on average with GFN1-xTB (paired 6.6), 1.6 with GFN2-xTB (paired 4.6); H + H₂ (measured 0.42 eV) GFN1 0.58 (paired 0.15), GFN2 0.03 (paired −0.40: H₃ bound); ozone losing an O (1.15 eV) GFN1 5.5 (7.6), GFN2 4.7 (6.9). Molecules without unpaired electrons (water, its dimer, the shared proton, HCN, hexazine) are unchanged. It costs ×1.2–1.6 where every electron is paired, ×1.6–2.3 among radicals.

Bond orders for metals

Metals

Normally metal atoms hold by pairs (Morse bonds), set so a metal melts near its real melting point. With Many-body, an atom’s bonding grows as the square root of its neighbours’ (the Finnis–Sinclair / Cleri–Rosato model): a lone pair is short and strong, surface atoms hold harder, and a vacancy costs a third of the cohesive energy. Each metal is fitted to its measured cohesive energy, spacing, bulk modulus and vacancy energy (copper: 3.49 eV, 2.56 Å, 137 GPa, 1.28 eV), reproduced exactly in 3-D. Alloys mix the two metals’ constants, with own fits for strongly bound pairs (NiAl, NiTi, Cu₃Au …); oxides and hydrides bond through the same form, fitted to measured compounds (Cu₂O, Al₂O₃, MgO, PdH …). The body-centred metals (W, Mo, Cr, V, Nb, Ta, Fe, Na) add directional terms so they crystallize bcc, as they really do. Small clusters bind too tightly (Cu₂ 2.9 eV, measured 2.0). Cost ×1–2 where there are metals. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath.

Flat sheets have six neighbours, not twelve, so copper holds 2.73 eV an atom at 2.45 Å and flat metals melt too high (Al ~1,000 K, real 933; Cu ~2,000 K, real 1,358). With directional bonding W, Mo and Cr sheets turn rectangular, Na square.

In 3-D a crystal has its twelve neighbours and the measured values come out exactly; a 739-atom copper ball melts from about 1,300 K. The body-centred metals sit at their measured bcc energy with fcc and hcp above it (W +0.47 eV), elastic constants mostly within 3–7%; the close-packed ones put bcc at its measured energy above them. Cost ×0.8–1.8 a step.

Directional forces

Lone pairs

With Lone pairs (3-D only), an oxygen with two single bonds carries its charge on two lone pairs 0.7 Å out, a nitrogen with three on one, as the TIP5P water model does, so water’s neighbours sit at a tetrahedron’s corners. Water dimer: bound 0.42 eV (point charges 0.21; measured 0.22); liquid water 4.5 neighbours (measured 4.3–4.7; point charges 6.0), and ice keeps its crystal at 250 K. Liquid water still comes out 17% too dense. Cost ×2–2.6. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath.

Halogen σ-holes

A halogen bonded to carbon is positive on the far tip of its bond (the σ-hole), where a lone pair can bind in line (a halogen bond). With σ-holes on, Cl, Br and I carry a small positive point beyond them on the bond axis. CH₃Br···NH₃: 1.9 kcal/mol at 179° (reference 1.7–2.0); CH₃I···NH₃: 2.9 (2.6–3.2). CF₃X binds 2–3× too hard. Cost ×1–2.7 where there are such halogens.

π-stacking

An aromatic ring’s π electrons make it negative on its faces and positive round its rim. With π clouds on (3-D only), each aromatic carbon carries charge points 0.7 Å above and below, and ring–ring dispersion is fitted to the benzene dimer. Benzene dimer: face to face −1.95, edge to face −2.84, offset −2.77 kcal/mol (CCSD(T): −1.7, −2.7, −2.7; without it −8.6, −4.5, −8.4). Cost up to ×10 in benzene.

More forces

Forces the game leaves out because at the scale of atoms they are tiny or rare, each its own switch (since 1.34). Most change nothing you could see at their real strength; that is the point of having them, and a boost (marked as such) is offered where it helps to see them work.

Gravity
Gravity's strength

Newton’s gravity between every pair of masses (real G), and optionally the Earth’s 9.81 m/s² down the screen. Between atoms it is 10⁻³⁶ of the Coulomb force, so at real strength nothing visibly changes; boost it ×10³³ and thirty argon atoms fall into a ball in a picosecond. Its energy is in the books.

A pushed or turned charge radiates (Larmor’s ⅔kq²a²/c³, with Liénard’s γ⁴ when fast): an electron circling a field gives off synchrotron light and spirals in. The energy leaves as photons along its path (at least 1 eV each) and is in the books. Invisible at thermal speeds; a 3 MeV electron in 20,000 T loses about a keV in 4 tu.

Every moving charge makes a magnetic field (Biot–Savart) that the other moving charges feel: parallel like charges pull together a little, opposite ones push apart. It does no work. Matters only for beams and plasmas. Flat game only.

Atomic magnets

Moments (gold arrows, flat game): atoms with unpaired electrons carry a magnetic moment (Fe 2.22 μB, Co 1.72, Ni 0.61, Gd 7.63) that precesses in the field it feels and, with the bath on, settles toward it with thermal noise. Exchange lines up neighbouring Fe, Co, Ni and Gd moments (J from each metal’s Curie temperature), so an iron cluster magnetizes itself below 1,043 K and loses it above.

Neutrinos meet matter

Neutrinos scatter off electrons by the measured weak cross sections (10⁻⁴⁵ cm² at 1 MeV), knocking them out of atoms. At the real rate a neutrino crosses a light-year of lead before it meets anything; the boosts multiply the cross section (×10²⁰: it hits the first molecule it meets). Absorption by nuclei is not built.

Partial charges are felt at every distance, not only within the force reach: the exact 1/r beyond it. Net charges already are (up to 220 of them, without wrapping walls). With wrapping walls, the nearest image of each pair.

Adds the three-body part of van der Waals attraction (D3’s Axilrod–Teller–Muto term) for every three atoms within 5 Å: it pushes apart close triangles and draws together lines. About 0.01 meV an atom in water, a few per cent of a noble-gas solid’s binding.

Light nuclei (up to oxygen) colliding above their Coulomb barrier and below 150 MeV a nucleon are followed nucleon by nucleon, held by a classical nuclear potential; the pieces they break into come out of the motion, the measured masses set the energy. Classical nucleons over-bind (⁴He 33 MeV, real 28.3). Fusion near the barrier keeps its measured rates. A corner shows the nucleons of the last collision.

Realistic particles

Particles shown as they really are, each part its own switch (since 1.27). Point at a line to see what it does.

Nuclei are hit at their true size, 1/100,000 of their atom, so beams rarely hit anything and nuclear reactions are rare (slow neutrons are still caught). Decays run at real rates, nothing sped up or held to be seen.

Nuclei are drawn at their true size too (a dot of one pixel marks where a bare nucleus or a free electron is). You can zoom in 100,000 times further, far enough to see a nucleus inside its atom, and slow time down to a yoctosecond (10⁻²⁴ s) a second, slow enough to watch a φ meson (1.5×10⁻²² s) live and decay.

In 3-D all of it applies, and zooming into an atom takes the camera past its electron cloud to its nucleus, nucleons and quarks at their true sizes. Time slows to a yoctosecond a second.

Each free electron and positron becomes a wave packet with a size and spin (the eFF model of Su and Goddard): squeezing it costs kinetic energy, and overlapping electrons push apart, hard with the same spin (Pauli). Free electrons crowd less and a dense electron gas holds itself up. With atoms and ions they still meet as points, so captures and ionization stay calibrated. Energy is in the books; forces match its slopes to 10⁻⁶ eV/Å.

Drawn as a faint disc. Costs ×6.5 in liquid water with 40 free electrons (their own steps 13 times shorter, for the packets' breathing, which takes a few hundredths of a femtosecond), nothing where there are none; that water keeps its energy to a few eV a picosecond (about as without).

In 3-D the packets meet in all three directions and are drawn as points. Cost: 40 free electrons among neon ×2.2.

Waves as

Full waves (flat game): each free electron or positron at 0.15–300 eV (up to six) carries a wave function on a 128² grid that moves with it, stepped by the split-operator method in the field of nearby atoms, so it spreads, interferes and tunnels. When found (in an atom’s core, at a wall, or spread too wide) it becomes a point with the wave’s energy. The books stay exact. Waves don’t feel each other or magnetic fields.

A slow μ⁻, π⁻, K⁻ (or, flat, p̄) is caught by an atom it passes, in proportion to Z (Fermi–Teller), takes an electron’s place and cascades down, giving off Auger electrons and X-ray lines (muonic lead 5.90 MeV, μp 1.9 keV). Then a muon decays or is captured by the nucleus at each element’s measured rate (2.0 µs in carbon, 75 ns in lead); a pion breaks the nucleus into a star; a kaon makes a Λ or Σ; an antiproton annihilates. A muonic atom bonds as the element one below (muonic carbon like boron). Muonic hydrogen is neutral and tiny, passing through atoms and handing its muon to heavier ones. Off: a muon caught at 2 keV, the others absorbed at once.

A muonic atom takes its element-one-below’s table values (valence, bonds, size, ionization energies). Not carried over: element-specific rules (muonic helium makes no hydrogen bonds), continuous bond orders and quantum bonds, and refilling of the cascade’s holes. The strong-force shift of pionic and kaonic lines is left out.

In the 3-D lab the same for muons, pions, kaons and antiprotons, in all three directions.

A slow positron takes an electron and becomes positronium (Ps): bound by 6.8 eV, twice hydrogen’s size. It forms from atoms in the Ore gap (hydrogen: 6.8–13.6 eV) or from slow free electrons. One in four is para-Ps: 125 ps, two 511 keV γ rays back to back. Three in four are ortho-Ps: 142 ns in vacuum, three γ rays in a plane; in matter it dies sooner by pick-off (1.8 ns in water), and O₂ turns ortho into para. Not modelled: its bubble in liquids, PsH. Off: a positron touching an electron annihilates.

Muonium (μ⁺ with an electron) is a light hydrogen: 0.114 u, bound 13.54 eV, bonding by hydrogen’s rules (MuH, MuCl), with large isotope effects (Mu–Cl vibrates 2.9× faster than H–Cl). The muon’s spin (μSR) precesses in a field: 135.5 MHz/T bare, 1.39 MHz/G in muonium; the decay positron leaves preferentially along the spin, so detectors around a sample see the precession. Inspect shows spin and frequency. Not modelled: radicals’ hyperfine frequencies, relaxation. Off: no spins, decay positrons isotropic.

Fast charged particles lose energy collision by collision: many small knocks and occasional hard ones that send out δ-ray electrons, so loss over a thin layer has Landau’s long tail. On average it is Bethe’s formula with each element’s measured excitation energy. Electrons radiate bremsstrahlung by each material’s radiation length (lead 5.6 mm, water 36 cm); γ rays above 1.022 MeV make e⁺e⁻ pairs against Compton scattering; faster than c/n in water or glass, Cherenkov light on a cone (41° in water). A nanometre box can’t show showers, and at nature’s rates radiation is rare here. Off: a smooth loss, simple bremsstrahlung, even pairs.

Matter depth

With Charged particles in matter on: speeds up its rare processes, bremsstrahlung and Cherenkov light, by the factor chosen, as if the box were that much deeper, so that in a box nanometres across fast electrons radiate, their γ rays make pairs (γ rays meet atoms at the game's high rate already) and a shower can start, and Cherenkov cones show. It speeds up only those rare processes: the collisions, δ-rays and energy loss stay at nature's rates, so a scaled shower is not nature's in its details. Its critical energy (where radiating overtakes the collisions: water 79 MeV, lead 8 MeV) comes out lower by about the same factor, and the radiation length shorter by it. ×1 is nature. Off (or Charged particles in matter off): nothing changes. Experimental: back to ×1 each time the game starts.

A resonance lives about 10⁻²³ s, so its mass spreads over its width Γ = ħ/τ. With this on, each resonance (ρ, ω, φ, K*, Δ, J/ψ, Υ) gets its own mass from the Breit–Wigner of its measured width (a ρ⁰ anywhere from 280 MeV up) and decays from it, paid for exactly. Hadron collisions above threshold make charm and bottom at the measured rates (cc̄: 1 in 700 at 40 GeV, 1 in 9 at 7 TeV) as D, Ds, Λc, B and Λb hadrons; most fly out of the box before decaying unless decay is sped up.

Three things nuclei do. Spin: each nucleus has its measured spin and moment (¹H ½, 2.793 μN; even–even nuclei none), precessing at its Larmor frequency (¹H 42.58 MHz/T); Inspect’s NMR pulse tips them and reads their signal. γ lines: excited nuclei give their measured lines (⁶⁰Co 1,173 + 1,332 keV, ¹³⁷Cs 662 keV via its 2.6 min isomer, H capture 2,224 keV), with conversion electrons and isomers (⁹⁹ᵐTc 6 h). Neutrons: measured thermal cross sections (²³⁵U fission 585 b, ¹¹³Cd 20,600 b, ¹⁵⁷Gd 254,000 b), 1/v and resonances. In ultra-realistic mode nuclei are hit at these cross sections; normally they decide what a meeting does.

Every photon carries a polarization. The Light tool and lasers shine polarized light; atoms, lamps and hot walls unpolarized; the two 511 keV γ rays of annihilation are polarized at right angles. Compton scattering follows Klein–Nishina with polarization. In the flat game mirrors keep it, a prism reflects by Fresnel’s laws (none across the plane at Brewster’s 58°), and the Objects tool has a polarizer (Malus’s law). Inspect shows each photon’s polarization. Not modelled: circular polarization, entanglement (the annihilation pair is a definite perpendicular pair: a weaker correlation than quantum mechanics gives). Off: no polarization.

Light that can’t ionize is scattered by molecules (Rayleigh, since 1.27): as 1/λ⁴, so blue scatters 9.4× as often as red (blue sky, red sunsets), more for more polarizable molecules (benzene 35× nitrogen), and polarized at right angles. The real rate is tiny in a nanometre box (80 km of air per scattering), so it is made visible: about 1 photon in 100 per nitrogen molecule at 450 nm, the ratios true. Ultra-realistic: the true rate. The photon keeps its energy.

Normally unstable particles meet only atoms, nuclei and colliding beams. With this on they meet in flight too: particle and antiparticle annihilate (μ⁺μ⁻ like e⁺e⁻; π⁺π⁻, K⁺K⁻ into mesons), other hadrons above threshold make new hadrons, and in the flat game charges turn each other. They meet within 0.35 Å (ultra-realistic: their true femtometre sizes, almost never). Products of one collision never meet. The books close exactly.

TeV proton collisions can make a Z′, leptoquarks and microscopic black holes, none of them ever found: at their predicted rates (ultra-realistic mode) about one collision in 10¹⁵, otherwise 10¹² times more often, labelled so. An e⁺e⁻ collider tuned to the Z′’s 6 TeV makes it at its real rate (since 1.39.3).

A free electron slowed below 1 eV in water settles into a cavity of water dipoles: the hydrated electron e⁻(aq), bound by about 1.7 eV (its 720 nm absorption makes irradiated water look blue). It diffuses (4.9×10⁻⁵ cm²/s) toward nearby ions and is captured by a cation within 2.5 Å. Light above 3.6 eV frees it. Its pull on the ions is not yet in the forces.

Light builds interference patterns one photon at a time (since 1.27, flat game). The Objects tool’s Slits (one, two or five openings) make fringes on a screen behind; X-rays scatter off crystals in Bragg’s directions (2d sin θ = mλ), off liquids into rings. X-rays of a few keV show it best; visible light’s wavelength is far larger than anything in the box. Not yet: thin films, coherence length, 3-D.

  • Lab → Condensate lab… : a Bose–Einstein condensate of ⁸⁷Rb, ²³Na or ⁷Li at nanokelvins, one wave function (Gross–Pitaevskii), flat or 3-D. Cool it through Tc, spin it for vortices, split and release it to see interference, or overload ⁷Li and watch it collapse.

What it does, and does not

  • A flat world (or a cube in 3-D) with real constants: atoms move classically under forces fitted to real chemistry; free electrons and nuclei are particles of their own; nuclear and particle physics come from measured data. Energy is booked exactly.
  • Simplified: an atom’s own electrons are a cloud, not particles; light travels as rays; very fast particles fly straight between collisions.
  • Known limits: highly ionized water gains 100–170 eV over a run; many thousands of atoms run slower than 1× on slower computers (Settings → Benchmark).
