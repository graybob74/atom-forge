Pair masses

Every pair of oppositely charged pions (or kaons, or a kaon and a pion) from one collision, by the mass they add up to (√(E² − p²) of the two together). Most pairs have nothing to do with each other and make a smooth background; pairs that came from one resonance all add up to its mass and stand out as a bump: the ρ at 775 MeV, the K* at 892, the φ at 1019 (and the K⁰s at 498, which lives long enough to fly a few cm first). Taking away the like-sign pairs (π⁺π⁺, π⁻π⁻), which no resonance makes, removes most of the background, as experiments do. This is how the resonances were discovered.

Charge

Charge is never made or lost. A positron that reaches a wall annihilates with one of the wall's electrons, leaving the wall one electron short (+1 e); a fast charged particle stopped in a wall leaves its charge there. So when light makes electron–positron pairs in a hot gas, the box ends up with extra electrons and the walls with as much positive charge. "All together" changes only when charge is added or removed from outside: what you place or remove, and beams.

What is simulated

Protons, neutrons, electrons, positrons, bare nuclei and atoms move in a two-dimensional chamberthree-dimensional box (the 3-D lab; the ordinary game is flat), in real units: ångströms (0.1 nm), femtoseconds and electron-volts. An atom is a nucleus carrying bound electrons; its electron count sets its charge, size, valence and spectrum. Free electrons are separate particles with the electron's real mass. At one temperature they move dozens of times faster than atoms, so they take many short time steps for each step of the atoms (multiple time stepping), and the step size everywhere adapts to the fastest motion and the stiffest contact.

In the 3-D lab

The same physics in a cube: molecules take their real shapes (tetrahedral carbon, bent water, VSEPR angles), double, aromatic and single bonds resist twisting, and every collision, decay, capture and nuclear reaction is worked out in three dimensions. Two of the flat world's corrections are still on here (van der Waals wells doubled, an ion's pull on water doubled; see Honest approximations), so liquids come out a little too dense. Not in 3-D yet: placed objects (mirrors, lenses, screens), chips, the collider and beam sources, antimatter; light is followed as rays, as in the flat game.

Forces

  • Coulomb's law between all charges, summed exactly at long range. Inside about one Bohr radius it is softened, as in the Kelbg/Deutsch potentials of plasma physics.
  • Van der Waals attraction and Pauli repulsion between atoms (Lennard-Jones with Universal Force Field parameters).
  • Hard cores. Closer than contact, atoms repel with the screened nuclear (Ziegler–Biersack–Littmark) potential, about 20 eV at 1 Å for U–H and hundreds of eV at 0.5 Å, so atoms can't be pushed through each other. Ligands around transition metals and f-block atoms repel one another directly. Atoms of the same molecule meet a softer wall, because a flat page crowds a molecule's groups far more than 3-D space does (force fields soften their 1-4 terms for a similar reason).
  • Born repulsion between ions, sized by Shannon ionic radii, and stiff enough to balance any pair of opposite charges near contact.
  • Directional forces (Settings → Experimental, since 1.26): lone pairs on oxygen and nitrogen (the 3-D lab), so hydrogen bonds point where the lone pairs do and water and ice make a tetrahedral network; σ-holes on chlorine, bromine and iodine, so a lone pair binds straight along a C–X bond (a halogen bond); π clouds above and below aromatic rings with a dispersion fitted to the benzene dimer (the 3-D lab), so rings stack offset or edge to face.
  • Covalent bonds as Morse springs with tabulated bond energies and lengths, plus VSEPR bond angles (104.5° in water, 107° in ammonia).
  • Partial charges from electronegativity differences, which is where hydrogen bonds come from. Formal charges spread the way resonance spreads them: the two oxygens of a carboxylate or nitro group share theirs, and opposite charges on neighbouring atoms largely cancel, so N₂O and ozone are only weakly polar, as they really are. Where double bonds can be drawn in more than one way (benzene, pyridine, naphthalene, anthracene, coronene), every way is counted and each bond takes the average, Pauling's bond order: 1.5 all round benzene (1.40 Å for C–C), 1⅔ and 1⅓ in naphthalene. The molecule is more stable than any one drawing by its resonance energy (150 kJ/mol for benzene, more with more drawings), so it holds its shape and resists adding atoms across its double bonds.
  • Metallic bonding calibrated so each metal melts near its real melting point. Settings → Experimental → Metals → Many-body (since 1.25) makes it many-body instead: each metal atom's bonding grows as the square root of how many neighbours it has (a tight-binding model fitted to each metal's measured cohesive energy, spacing, stiffness and vacancy energy), so a lone pair of atoms is short and strong, surface atoms hold harder to each neighbour, and an atom pulled out of a crystal costs less than all its bonds. Since 1.26 oxygen and hydrogen atoms bond to such a metal in the same model (fitted to measured oxides and hydrides: Cu₂O, Al₂O₃, FeO, MgO, NaH and others), so O₂ splits on a metal and its surface oxidizes, and an ion near a metal feels the pull of its image charge; strongly bound alloys (Ni–Al, Ni–Ti, Fe–Al, Cu–Zn, Cu–Au, Ti–Al) have constants of their own fitted to their measured compounds; and the body-centred metals (W, Mo, Cr, V, Nb, Ta, Fe, Na) bond with direction, so they crystallize body-centred cubic.
  • Lorentz force on moving charges. Free electrons move on exact circular arcs between force kicks, so cyclotron orbits and the E×B drift across a field come out exact at any field strength, and a strong field does not force tiny time steps. Field gradients pull ferromagnets (below their Curie point) and paramagnets, and push diamagnets, with the matching magnetic energy counted in the totals.
  • Electroweak. Above about 100 GeV electromagnetism and the weak force are one force: the photon and the W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰ bosons carry it. Below, the W and Z are heavy (80.4 and 91.2 GeV, almost a hundred proton masses), because of the Higgs field, and the weak force is weak only because they are so heavy to borrow; the photon has no mass and reaches far. The W and Z are made for real only in collisions above about 80–90 GeV (since 1.27; see Accelerators). The Higgs field fills all space with the same value, and gives the W, Z, quarks and charged leptons their masses (the game takes those masses as measured numbers: the field is the same in every atom and collision, so a box has nothing of it to follow). Its particle, the Higgs boson (125.2 GeV, since 1.27), is made in collisions above about 125 GeV: at the LHC mostly by two gluons fusing (about 56 pb at 13 TeV, one collision in 1.4 billion), and at an e⁺e⁻ collider above 216 GeV as e⁺e⁻ → Z H (0.24 pb at 250 GeV; LEP reached 209 GeV, just short). It lives 1.6×10⁻²² s and decays where it is made: b b̄ 58%, W W* 21% (one W virtual), two gluons 8%, τ τ 6%, c c̄ 3%, Z Z* 2.6%, γ γ 0.23%, Z γ 0.15%, μ μ 0.02%.

Chemistry rules

  • Valence from the electron configuration (Aufbau with the Cr/Cu-type exceptions), the octet and duet rules, hypervalence for period-3+ atoms bonded to F, O or Cl.
  • Two radicals bond only if a third body can carry away the bond energy.
  • Atom-transfer reactions need collision energy above an Evans–Polanyi activation barrier; transition metals act as catalysts by lowering it.
  • Electrons hop between atoms when the Born–Haber balance (ionization energy, electron affinity, electrostatics) says it releases energy: that is ionic bonding. Bare protons attach to lone pairs (H₃O⁺, NH₄⁺).
  • Light breaks bonds it has the energy for (photodissociation); infrared light heats polar molecules.
  • Molecules are named by structure, which atoms are joined to which, so isomers such as ethanol and dimethyl ether, or butane and isobutane, each get their own name.

Atomic physics

  • Measured ionization energies and electron affinities for the outer electrons; Dirac's formula for the last one (uranium's is 132 keV deep); Slater's rules between, scaled so all of an atom's electrons together are bound by the measured total (760 keV for uranium).
  • Each ion's Coulomb pull on a free electron is softened over a core sized so the classical well is exactly as deep as the real level the electron would be captured into. A classical electron can then never be more tightly bound than quantum mechanics allows.
  • Electron capture by ions releases the binding energy as light: hydrogen cascades down its levels emitting Lyman, Balmer (656, 486, 434, 410 nm) and Paschen lines.
  • Orbitals (the Orbitals view, O, and the Inspect tab): each occupied subshell is a hydrogen-like wavefunction, with its radial nodes and angular shape, screened by Slater's rules and filled by Hund's rule from the atom's live configuration. The chamber shows each atom's electron density in a slice through its nucleus; s, p, d and f orbitals are drawn blue, amber, green and pink.
  • Quantum solver (Inspect tab, on request): a self-consistent field calculation of the selected atom or ion, Hartree–Fock–Slater with Latter's correction as in Herman and Skillman's tables. Each electron's radial Schrödinger equation is solved numerically in the field of the nucleus and the others' average charge, repeated until the field stops changing (about 25 rounds, tens of milliseconds). It gives every orbital's energy and size and the shell structure of the charge, the outermost orbital's energy comes within 1 eV of the measured ionization energy on average (3 eV at worst: nitrogen's half-filled shell, and gold and mercury, where relativity matters), and it finds that ions such as O²⁻ hold no bound state for their last electron. Once run, the Orbitals view draws that species from its orbitals.
  • Ions shed electrons they cannot hold (anything beyond a stable anion; doubly charged atomic anions do not exist in empty space). The electron tunnels out through the ion's own repulsion, carrying the energy its departure releases, so the books still balance.
  • Ionization by electron impact, by hot collisions, by strong fields and by photons (photoelectric effect). Hot atoms glow with their own emission lines (flame tests) and with blackbody colour.
  • Gamma rays Compton-scatter, make electron–positron pairs above 1.022 MeV, and positrons annihilate into two 511 keV photons.

Placed objects (Objects tool, D)

  • Optics. Mirrors reflect light (angle in equals angle out); a beam splitter sends each photon one way or the other at random, half and half. A thin lens bends a ray crossing it at height h by h/f. A prism of F2 flint glass (n = 1.648 at 400 nm, 1.609 at 700 nm) refracts by Snell's law at each face, so violet bends more than red; met straight on it reflects totally inside, as a real one does. Glass absorbs ultraviolet below 300 nm; X-rays and γ rays go straight through all of them.
  • Detector screen and beam stop. Both stop light and fast particles (their energy goes into them, out of the chamber's books); antimatter annihilates there. The screen counts what hits it and draws its energy spectrum along itself.
  • Walls. Atoms meet every object as a soft wall on their edge, as at the chamber's walls (the energy is kept in the books); electrons bounce off. A wall object turns fast particles back too.
  • Foil. Fast charged particles cross a few nanometres of metal: at each atomic layer they pass some nucleus at a random distance b and are turned by 2·atan(d/2b), d = kzZ/E (Rutherford). Most go nearly straight; a few, passing very close, bounce back, which is how the nucleus was found (Geiger and Marsden, 1909). Ones too slow to get through stop in it.
  • Charged plates. A uniform field V/gap between two plates; outside them, none. Like the Lab's electric field, the energy it gives comes from the supply.

Nuclear physics

  • Binding energies from the Atomic Mass Evaluation: tabulated up to zinc, and for the heavy decay chains (thorium, uranium, plutonium …) worked out from their measured decay energies, down to the stable lead and bismuth they end in. Elsewhere the semi-empirical mass formula, which overbinds the actinides by about 13 MeV. Q-values follow from them; a uranium-235 fission gives its fragments about 170 MeV and prompt γ rays about 8 MeV, as measured.
  • Real half-lives and decay modes for tabulated nuclides; otherwise Viola–Seaborg (α), Fermi-integral (β) and fissility (spontaneous fission) estimates. β particles get their real spectrum up to the end-point energy (for β⁺, two electron masses below the atomic Q-value).
  • A decaying nucleus keeps its electrons, so an α-decay daughter has two too many (and a β⁺ daughter one): they are shaken off at once, as in real decays. Each decay's caption shows it up close (what leaves the nucleus and what it becomes), and the Inspect tab charts a radioactive nucleus's whole decay chain on the chart of the nuclides.
  • Fusion by quantum tunnelling: Gamow factor times each reaction's astrophysical S-factor. Above 150 keV in their own frame (where the D + T collider's beams meet) D–T follows its measured cross section, which falls about 25-fold by 4.8 MeV (0.046 c each); there touching nuclei mostly bounce off or break up, and ⁴He + n comes from about 1 pass in 100.
  • Neutron capture, fission, (n,p) and (n,α) chosen by cross-section ratios; fission fragments follow the double-humped yield curve and release 2–3 neutrons. Above 20 MeV a neutron flies at its relativistic speed (a 2 GeV one at 0.95 c), and a hit is a nuclear collision like any fast nucleus's: spallation, and for heavy nuclei usually fission.
  • Antimatter obeys the same rules with every charge reversed (CPT symmetry): antiprotons bind positrons into antihydrogen with hydrogen's spectrum, and antiatoms bond like atoms. Where matter touches antimatter, an electron and a positron annihilate into two 511 keV γ rays, and a nucleon and an antinucleon into mesons (about five pions, now and then kaons, 1.88 GeV in all), after a cascade that shakes out a few electrons and knocks out nucleons. The energy comes exactly from the rest masses destroyed.

Accelerators

  • Ring collider (a synchrotron). The ring is built as real ones are: eight bending magnets (45° each) with quadrupole magnets between them, focusing (F) and defocusing (D) in turn. A string of alternating lenses focuses overall (alternating-gradient or strong focusing), so a particle off the orbit swings back and forth about it (betatron oscillations, here about 2.2 per turn). Straight sections between the magnets hold the RF cavities, separators, kickers and detectors. All the magnets follow the beams' momentum as they are sped up, B = p/(qρ) for the bending magnets and the quadrupoles in proportion, as in a real ramp.
  • Injection. A small linac at each injection point brings a train of particles up to the injection energy along a line coming in from inside the ring, and a kicker magnet, fired as the train passes, turns it onto the orbit. Electrons and positrons go in at 1.5 MeV, above transition (see below), or at full energy if that is lower, so they never have to cross it (real electron rings also inject well above it); other beams go in at a twentieth of their final energy.
  • RF cavities and bunches. The cavities' field oscillates four times per turn (harmonic number h = 4), locked to the beam's revolution. The ideal (synchronous) particle meets them at the phase φs where they give it just the energy the ramp calls for, qV·sin φs. A particle arriving late sees a different phase: below transition, where a faster particle goes round sooner, φs sits on the rising side of the wave, so a late particle gains more and catches up and an early one gains less and falls back. So the train is gathered into a bunch whose particles swing to and fro about the synchronous one (synchrotron oscillations), inside a region of phase and energy called the RF bucket; particles outside it drift away, fall behind the rising field and are lost. Above transition (γ above 1/√αc ≈ 2.1, set by how much longer the orbit gets when the momentum rises), a faster particle swings out onto a longer orbit and takes longer to go round, so φs moves to the falling side of the wave. The voltage is programmed for 12% more energy each turn; at full energy the bucket only holds the bunch (and gives electrons back what they radiate).
  • One ring or two. A particle and its antiparticle (e⁺ and e⁻, p and p̄) have the same mass and opposite charges, so one ring of magnets bends both inward while they go round opposite ways (as at LEP and the Tevatron), and one RF system speeds up both. While they are sped up, electrostatic separators at the four corners put them on orbits a few ångström apart where they cross (pushing outward at the upper corners and inward at the lower ones, so each orbit keeps its length and both beams stay in step with the RF). At full energy the separators are turned off and the beams collide at both detectors. Any other pair, beams of one charge (D + T, gold on gold) or of different masses, needs a ring each (as at the LHC and RHIC): here two rings side by side that share one straight, where the detector sits. The two beams fly at the same speed, each one's energy in proportion to its mass (the same energy per nucleon), so their bunches take the same time per turn and meet in the detector every turn. While they are sped up the bending magnets are set a little strong, which pulls both orbits in, away from the shared straight. Each beam can also be given its own energy (as in the asymmetric B factories, PEP-II and KEKB): a particle and its antiparticle then need a ring each, since the same magnets cannot bend both, and beams at different speeds take different times per turn, so their bunches meet in the detector only when their timings line up.
  • Keeping the beams apart. By default the beams are kept apart while they are sped up and brought together at full energy. They can instead be kept apart to go round without colliding, or brought together at once. Brought together early, they pull on each other harder: the beam–beam kick falls as 1/(p·v), so at a tenth of their final energy the bunches pull about ten times harder and can blow each other apart. This beam–beam limit is why real colliders keep their beams apart until full energy.
  • Beam–beam forces. As two bunches cross, each particle is pushed or pulled by the other bunch's charge: a charge passing at distance b gives a sideways kick Δp = 2k·q₁q₂(1 + β₁β₂)/(b·v), its electric field and, for beams meeting head-on, its magnetic field too. Opposite charges (e⁺e⁻, pp̄) pinch each other; like charges push apart. Each beam particle here stands for many real ones, as many as give a kick of a few hundredths of a radian at the bunch's edge, about a real collider's beam–beam strength.
  • Synchrotron radiation. A charge bent on a circle radiates P = (2/3)·ke²cγ⁴β⁴/ρ², so an electron loses (4π/3)(ke²/ρ)γ⁴β³ each turn. Bent on a 2.4 nm radius that is about 2.5 eV × γ⁴: under 2 keV per turn at 2 MeV, but near 17 MeV (γ ≈ 34) some 3.5 MeV, as much as the cavities can give. So electrons are sped up only to 14.6 MeV, 85% of that, where the RF still holds them in their bunches. Protons radiate (mp/me)⁴ ≈ 10¹³ times less.
  • Real-world scale. The collider panel works out what these beams would need in real hardware: at the LHC's 8.3 T the bending radius p/(qB) would be millimetres to metres for these beams, where here it is 2.4 nm, so the magnets here need 10⁵ T and more, and the RF runs at petahertz frequencies instead of a few hundred megahertz.
  • Linear collider. Each linac pushes its beam through a set voltage, and a quadrupole magnet just before the collision point focuses it. (A real quadrupole focuses in one direction across the beam and defocuses in the other; a flat world has only the first.)
  • What collides: beam particles that pass within 0.9 Å of each other (about their drawn size, or more for big nuclei), in a ring once the beams are brought together at full energy. The pass is mapped onto real impact parameters as an area, out to where Coulomb scattering turns them by about 4° (or the nuclei's contact distance, if larger). An electron and a positron annihilate; matter and antimatter annihilate into mesons; nuclei that touch above their Coulomb barrier react, and light ones tunnel and fuse (Gamow factor) below it. Otherwise the two scatter off each other's charge by Rutherford's law in their own frame: a dead-on pass turns them right round, a glancing one by a few degrees. Relativistic kinematics throughout: the beams fly at their true speeds, which is why the view goes into slow motion. Beams can be given up to 10 GeV per particle.
  • Unstable particles as beams. Any charged particle that lives long enough to steer can be a beam: muons and τ leptons, charged pions and kaons, the Σ⁺, Σ⁻, Ξ⁻ and Ω⁻ hyperons, the D⁺, D⁺s and B⁺ mesons, and their antiparticles. They go round through the same magnets and cavities, radiate as any charge does (γ⁴, so a muon 207⁴ times less than an electron of the same energy), and decay on their own clocks, stretched by time dilation: a 1 GeV muon would go round some 10¹¹ times first, a 5 GeV τ about 9,000 (the collider's note gives the number for the beams chosen). A muon and an antimuon annihilate as an electron and a positron do; a hadron and its antihadron annihilate into mesons; a hadron meets a proton or nucleus beam as it would in matter; two other hadrons come through with new hadrons made between them, as often as protons do at the same energy above their masses; anything else scatters off the other's charge.
  • New particles. An electron and a positron annihilate into whatever their energy in their own frame, √s, allows, in proportion to the cross sections: two γ rays (always the most likely), muon pairs above 211 MeV, pion pairs (through the ρ), the φ at 1.02 GeV (mostly kaon pairs), hadron showers above about 1.1 GeV, τ pairs above 3.55 GeV. Electron rings top out at 14.6 MeV (synchrotron light), so these need the linear collider, which is why real e⁺e⁻ colliders beyond LEP are planned as linacs. At √s = 91.2 GeV (45.6 GeV a beam) nearly every collision makes a Z⁰ (since 1.27): σ = 59 nb on the peak (hadrons 41.5 nb, μμ 2.0 nb), 2.5 GeV wide, the Z decaying where it is made into two jets of hadrons (70%), a lepton pair (3.4% each) or two neutrinos that nothing sees (20%: how LEP counted three neutrino kinds); the muon pairs lean forward below the peak and backward above it (the photon and Z interfering), and a W's charged lepton follows the weak force's handedness. Above 161 GeV (80.4 GeV a beam) W⁺W⁻ pairs (17 pb), above 182 GeV ZZ (1 pb), as at LEP2. Above 216.4 GeV (108.2 GeV a beam) Z H, a Z giving off a Higgs boson (Higgs-strahlung: 0.24 pb at 250 GeV, since 1.27), what a future Higgs factory would make; LEP stopped at 209 GeV. The setup CERN LEP: the Z factory does this. Hadron collisions make Ws and Zs too, at their measured rates: at the Tevatron's 1.96 TeV one proton–antiproton collision in 2.4 million makes a W, one in 8 million a Z, so a run here almost never shows one. The Fire tool reaches them through hadrons: a proton or pion fired into matter at its top energy, 1 PeV, meets each nucleon at √s ≈ 1.4 TeV, where about one hit in a few million makes a W or Z. Not through electrons: a positron meeting an electron at rest has √s = √(2mₑE), only 32 GeV at 1 PeV (the Z peak would need 8 PeV), and fired particles this fast cross the box in one step.
  • Unstable particles (muons, pions, kaons, η, Λ, τ) fly at their true speeds, curve in magnetic fields (neutral ones don't, and leave dotted tracks) and decay in flight with their real mean lives stretched by time dilation (γτ), on the same clock as radioactive decay. A π⁰ lives 8.4×10⁻¹⁷ s, about 25 nm of flight, so it decays in the chamber into two γ rays; charged pions, kaons and muons live nanoseconds to microseconds and leave, as they do in a real detector, unless decay is sped up (Lab › Time), when π → μ → e chains appear. Charged ones lose energy ionizing the atoms they pass. Hadrons meet the nuclei they pass (where the nuclei are drawn, as nucleons do): in the outer part of the reach they scatter elastically, nearer in they strike one nucleon (with its Fermi motion), so a pion is absorbed on a nucleon pair or makes more pions, a K⁻ swaps its strangeness (K̄N → Λπ), a K⁺ scatters or swaps charge, a hyperon knocks a nucleon out (or a Σ turns into a Λ), and an antibaryon annihilates into pions and kaons; the struck nucleus is left excited and boils off nucleons. A negative pion, Σ⁻, Ξ⁻ or Ω⁻ that stops is caught by an atom and absorbed by its nucleus (π⁻p → π⁰n or γn; on heavier nuclei on a nucleon pair, breaking the nucleus up; Σ⁻p → Λn), and a Λ left slow in the nucleus may stay, making a hypernucleus. Free neutrons scatter off each other, and a nucleus moving into a neutron meets it as surely as a neutron moving into the nucleus. Fast electrons radiate as they swerve past nuclei (bremsstrahlung, about T·Z/800 MeV of their collision losses: an X-ray tube's anode), γ rays above about 8 MeV can knock a nucleon out of a nucleus (the giant dipole resonance), and a stopped μ⁺ often takes an electron to make muonium, a hydrogen atom with a muon for its proton.
  • New elements. Nuclei that touch above their Coulomb barrier react, chosen by their energy per nucleon: light systems (16 nucleons or fewer) break up into nucleons and light nuclei (Fermi's statistical breakup). Heavier ones fuse into a hot compound nucleus that cools by boiling off neutrons, protons and α particles, each with an evaporation-spectrum energy, or fissions (Weisskopf's and Bohr and Wheeler's statistical model), so ¹²C + ¹²C makes ²⁰Ne, ²³Na and their neighbours. Pairs heavier than Z₁Z₂ ≈ 1,700 mostly part again before fusing (quasi-fission), which is why superheavy elements form only once in billions of tries. Glancing passes trade nucleons (deep-inelastic). Above about 40 MeV per nucleon the overlapping parts burst into nucleons, light nuclei and, with energy enough, pions, while the rest of each nucleus flies on hot and evaporates (fragmentation; for a nucleon on a nucleus, spallation). About a third of touching passes at high energy are diffraction instead (nuclear elastic scattering at a small angle). The new nuclei then decay as any others do.
  • Exact books. Every collision shares its energy and momentum among its products by relativistic phase space, so both are kept exactly; rest mass made or destroyed is entered in the nuclear ledger and photons and neutrinos take what is left to the last eV.

Honest approximations

  • Two dimensions. Densities, neighbour counts and the Maxwell–Boltzmann law are 2D, and van der Waals well depths are doubled to make up for the missing neighbours. Melting and boiling points are therefore only roughly right. Molecules are flat too: a tetrahedral carbon becomes a cross with 90° angles, and 3-D shapes such as the cyclohexane chair are pressed flat. The 3-D lab (main menu → 3-D, a preview) puts atoms and molecules in a cube instead, with their real 3-D angles, for chemistry, liquids and gases, with fields, magnets, light and unstable particles (pions, kaons, muons) too, and what they do to nuclei: hits on nuclei, captures when they stop (muonic atoms, nuclear stars, hypernuclei) and muonium; and nuclear physics between atoms: radioactive decay, neutrons (capture, fission), fusion and reactions of fast nuclei (a heavy nucleus fired at hundreds of GeV a nucleon makes a quark–gluon plasma), γ rays knocking out neutrons; the collider, antimatter, placed objects and chips are in the flat game only for now.
  • Ions in water pull twice as hard. In a flat world an ion has about half the solvent around it that it has in space (3–4 water molecules against 6), and the solvent screens charge far less, so with the plain Coulomb force salt would never dissolve and an Na⁺ and a Cl⁻ in water would never part. The pull between a free ion and a polar molecule's partial charges is doubled to make up for it: an ion's shell then holds it about as strongly as real water does, a lone ion pair parts, and a crystal dissolves from its corners. Energy is conserved as before. A side effect: salts that barely dissolve in real water because their bonding is partly covalent (silver chloride) dissolve here like table salt.
  • Electrons are not solved for as waves while atoms move. Bound electrons are book-kept (shells, configurations, energies). The orbitals the Orbitals view draws come from that bookkeeping, with Slater's rules, which overestimate the size of heavy atoms' outer s orbitals, until the quantum solver has been run for a species; either way they are pictures and steer nothing. The solver includes relativity as an average over each orbital's two spin–orbit partners (gold's 6s comes out 8.3 eV deep against the measured 9.2 eV ionization energy, and 16% smaller than without it; without relativity it was 6.5 eV), but not the spin–orbit splitting itself nor the electrons' spins, and treats one atom at a time: real orbitals also mix into molecular orbitals when atoms bond, which nothing here computes, except Settings → Experimental → Bond orders → Quantum (GFN1-xTB, or GFN2-xTB since 1.26; with each spin's own orbitals since 1.26 under Spins → Polarized), which works out the molecular orbitals of up to 40 atoms at every step. Free electrons are classical point charges, unless Settings → Experimental → Electrons as waves makes them wave packets (eFF: a size, a spin, the Pauli principle between them); even then a packet meets ions and atoms as a smeared charge, not as a solved wave.
  • Rule-based chemistry. A bond's energy depends on the element pair and bond order, not the rest of the molecule (every O–H bond is the same), so some reaction heats are off by tens of kJ/mol.
  • Enlarged nuclei. A nucleus is hit where it is drawn: its size in the Nucleus view, 0.045 Å per nucleon across (uranium's is 0.8 Å), and never less than 0.25–0.35 Å, instead of a few femtometres, so fusion, capture and fission happen in a nanometre box. A pass inside it is mapped onto real impact parameters, so relative rates keep their Gamow and cross-section scaling. A fast electron crossing a nucleus is turned by its full charge (at a few MeV it bounces back).
  • Collisions shown simply. Above a few GeV, products come out along the collision axis, from strings breaking into hadrons (electron–positron pairs as two jets back to back); below that, they share their momenta over phase space; all of it in two dimensions; charm and bottom quarks become D and B mesons only in electron–positron collisions (in hadron and nuclear collisions, which make them far more rarely, they are left out); electrons don't excite nuclei (it takes a pass far closer than any here); unstable particles lose energy to the atoms they pass and are turned a little by each nucleus they pass (multiple scattering; in the flat chamber a path meets close nuclei more often than in 3-D, so it spreads several times wider than through a real foil).
  • Tiny objects, flattened. The chips, proteins, DNA, membrane and crystals of the molecule library lie flat like everything else: silicon's four bonds at right angles, a protein's side chains to either side of its chain, the DNA double helix unwound into a ladder. The chips' dopant atoms are far denser than in real ones, so that a few show at all, and the wells of a logic gate are parted by a single seam of oxygen where real chips have a trench of oxide.
  • Sped-up decay. Motion and radioactive decay share one clock, which runs at about a picosecond per second of watching. A 14 MeV neutron really does cross the box in a few femtoseconds, and in a box this small most neutrons and gamma rays escape. At real rates almost nothing decays in that time, so decays can be sped up (Lab › Time); the factor is shown whenever it is on, and slow motion after a nuclear event slows motion and decay together. Decay can also be slowed, down to a millionth of its real rate, so short-lived particles fly further and their tracks can be followed; and the very shortest-lived (resonances, which live 10⁻²³ s) can be held until they have flown a few ångströms. Both are shown while on: they change where decays happen, not what they make.
  • Huge fields. Bending thermal ions within nanometres takes about 10⁴ tesla; laboratory magnets reach about 45 T.
  • Shrunken accelerators. The rings are 11 nm across, so their magnets need 10⁵ T and more and their RF petahertz frequencies (real synchrotrons: about 8 T over kilometres, and a few hundred megahertz), and the beams gain 12% of their energy each turn instead of a fraction of a percent. The beams are flat: quadrupoles focus only across the ring. A bunch is a few particles that ignore each other (no space charge) and stand for many in the beam–beam forces. The separated orbits are only a few ångström apart, not the many beam widths of a real machine, so beams are kept from colliding until they are brought together at full energy. Beam particles collide when they pass within 0.9 Å, far more than the nuclear reach, so collisions are far more frequent than in a real beam.
  • Energy is conserved. Every event (bonding, reaction, ionization, capture, electron transfer, decay, fusion, fission) balances kinetic, potential and electronic energy exactly; the energy a nuclear event releases from mass includes the change in its electrons' binding, as real atomic masses do. With the bath set to Insulated the total energy stays constant to within integration error, and light leaving the box is counted in the ledger.

Shorter longest step

Longest time step

Calm matter is moved on in steps of 0.4 fs (busy scenes already take shorter ones). Shorter steps keep the energy books a little more exact.

In the flat game liquid water drifts 0.07 eV/ps at 0.2 fs and 0.06 at 0.1 fs, against 0.17; salt water −0.44 and −0.22 against −0.56. Costs ×1.6–1.8 and ×2.6–2.9 where the steps are at their longest; nothing in hot plasmas and ionized water, whose steps are shorter anyway.

In the 3-D lab a step costs about the same whatever its length, so the cost follows the number of steps: ×2 at 0.2 fs and ×3.3–4.1 at 0.1 fs (liquid water, salt water; measured in 3-D, 1.25), nothing where the steps are shorter anyway.

Resonance in large ring systems

Bonds that can be drawn more than one way take the average over every way (1.5 all round benzene). Up to 40 atoms the ways are counted one by one; past that the six-membered rings are simply given 1.5. With this on, the bond orders of large flat ring systems (graphene flakes, big polycyclic molecules, up to 1,000 ring atoms) are worked out exactly from a determinant instead (the same numbers as counting, where both can be done), and their resonance energy grows with them. Costs nothing while bonds stay put; each time such a molecule's bonding changes, 12 ms at 200 ring atoms, 60 ms at 500, 0.2 s at 1,000.

Detailed nuclear collisions

A proton or neutron striking a nucleus (heavier than oxygen, 40 MeV to 10 GeV) is followed through it nucleon by nucleon (an intranuclear cascade): elastic scattering, pions made, collisions the Pauli principle forbids; fast nucleons and pions get out, the rest heats the nucleus, which then boils off neutrons, protons and α or splits. Instead of the statistical model (a fireball of the nucleons in its path). 1 GeV protons on lead: 14.9 neutrons a reaction (measured about 17; the statistical model 13.9), 145 MeV left in the nucleus (measured 150–200), fission 21% (measured about 10%), and about 1.4 pions (pions caught again on the way out are not followed). About 3 ms a hit instead of 1; nuclear events take 1–5% of the time in the nuclear setups, so it hardly shows.

The flat game only: in the 3-D lab nucleons do not strike nuclei yet (nuclear reactions between atoms are in the flat game only), so it changes nothing there. It stays set for the flat game.

Longer force reach

Force reach

Van der Waals and partial-charge forces stop at 6 Å (net charges reach farther while there are few of them). A longer reach keeps more of the pull between molecules. It runs the JavaScript force kernels (the WebAssembly ones have 6 Å built in). The potential energy changes when the reach does: the energy books step once.

Measured in the flat game on the same arrangement, liquid water binds 0.9 kJ/mol a molecule more at 9 Å and 1.6 at 12 Å (of about 41 kJ/mol to boil it off), brine 8.5 and 11. Costs ×1.3–2.6 at 9 Å and ×1.6–5.6 at 12 Å (brine, liquid water, 4,000 waters), more in big boxes.

In space the partners within reach grow as the cube of the reach (in the flat game as its square), so it costs far more in the 3-D lab: ×3.5–5 at 9 Å and ×7–17 at 12 Å (salt water, liquid water; measured in 3-D, 1.25).

Charges that respond to their surroundings

Induced charges

Each molecule's partial charges are its bonds' own, plus induced charges that follow the electric field around it from other molecules, ions and an applied field (charge equilibration). No charge moves between molecules, so a water molecule stays neutral but turns its dipole toward an ion or a field. The polarization energy is part of the energy books, and the energy stays exact. The charges lag between updates, which warms an insulated box a little. Molecules over 64 atoms keep their own charges.

Fitted to water's measured polarizability (1.45 Å³, averaged in the plane); in liquid water the molecules' dipole rises from 2.19 to 2.46 D, and next to a sodium ion much more. Every 10th step costs ×1.4–1.8 and every step ×2.7–4.6 (brine, liquid water, 4,000 waters).

In the 3-D lab the charges follow the field in all three directions, with the flat game's constants (fitted to water's measured 1.45 Å³ in the plane); in liquid water the induced charges average 0.09 e (the flat water's 0.06). Every 10th step costs ×1.4–2.4 and every step ×2.5–3.2 (liquid water, salt water; measured in 3-D, 1.26: the pairs are found in WebAssembly).

Polarizability

With Induced charges on (since 1.27). Normally the induced charges flow along the bonds, so a molecule polarizes only along its bonds: N₂, CO₂ and benzene not at all across them (out of benzene's plane nothing), and benzene's ring about 14 times too much. By direction gives each atom an induced dipole instead, which follows the field from other molecules, ions and an applied field with the polarizability its bonds have along and across them (bond polarizabilities fitted to 16 measured molecules), so every molecule polarizes in each direction as much as the real one: N₂ 2.20 / 1.51 Å³ along / across, CO₂ 3.96 / 1.96, benzene 12.2 in its plane and 6.6 out of it, water 1.47, methane 2.60. The dipoles are worked out together (each one feels the others'), softened at close range as Thole's model does so they cannot run away, and the energy books stay exact. Molecules of 2–64 atoms; a lone atom or ion is not polarized.

In the flat game, in the plane. In liquid water the molecules' dipole comes out 2.5–2.6 D (charges alone 2.17, induced charges 2.4). Costs ×1.8–2.9 on top of the induced charges (liquid water, brine; measured 1.27).

In liquid water the molecules' dipole comes out 2.6–2.7 D (charges alone 2.2, induced charges 2.4–2.5; measured in the liquid about 2.6–3.0 D). Costs ×1.2–4.2 on top of the induced charges (liquid water, brine; measured in 3-D, 1.27).

Quantum nuclei

Quantum nuclei
Bead forces

Atoms are not points: a nucleus, light ones most, is spread out by its zero-point motion and can tunnel through barriers. With this on every atom is a ring of beads (path-integral molecular dynamics), each feeling the forces at its own copy of the world. The heat bath keeps the rings at its temperature. Free electrons, bare nuclei and fast particles stay points. Bead forces, contracted (since 1.21, ring-polymer contraction): the bonds and bends, stiff and short, where the spread matters, are worked out on every bead; the pairs between atoms, smooth over a ring's tenth of an ångström, once at the rings' centres. Continuous bond orders and electrons as waves always take every bead.

Zoomed in, the beads show as small dots over each atom. At 300 K the H₂ bond spreads from 0.037 to 0.089 Å wide (16 beads) and lengthens from 0.746 to 0.764 Å (measured 0.766); heavy hydrogen spreads less, from the masses alone. In an insulated box the rings need shorter steps: liquid water drifts 0.27 eV in 30 fs at the usual step, 0.07 at 0.2 fs (Longest time step above). Liquid water keeps its quantum spread (H 0.10 Å) and its bond lengths to 0.003 Å against every bead. Costs on liquid water: ×1.4 (8 beads), ×3 (16), ×6 (32) contracted; ×3.3, ×7, ×16 with the whole force field on every bead.

In the 3-D lab the rings spread in all three directions (among 27 waters a hydrogen's ring is 0.16 Å across, 0.09 Å in depth alone: the same every way), drawn round each atom when you come close. Costs on liquid water in 3-D (1,029 atoms, 1.25): ×1.6 (8 beads), ×2.7 (16), ×6 (32) contracted; ×8, ×17, ×29 with the whole force field on every bead (after 1.25; ×17, ×30, ×48 before the neighbour list served every bead).

Continuous bond orders

Bond orders

Normally a bond is stored with a whole-number order, and bonds form, break and swap partners as events, each with its measured energy. With this on, neutral molecules of non-metals (H, B, C, N, O, F, Si, P, S, Cl, Br, I, and since 1.25 Ge, As, Se, Sn, Sb, Te: tin in its molecules such as SnCl₄, while tin metal keeps its metallic bonding) keep no stored bonds: every pair's bond order is worked out at every step from where the atoms are, so a bond stretches, weakens and breaks continuously, new ones grow, and a reaction finds its own barrier (H + H₂: 0.41 eV, measured 0.42). Statics match the stored bonds within 0.04 eV a bond. Their partial charges follow the orders (as a stored bond's do), so they hydrogen-bond: liquid water keeps half its H atoms in hydrogen bonds (stored bonds 57%). Atoms that can't bond to each other push apart as stored molecules' atoms do (since 1.19: before, two waters drew together to 2.14 Å between their oxygens, now 2.79, as with stored bonds); atoms that both have valence free meet without that wall, so radicals still join freely. Molecular ions (H₃O⁺, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, nitrate) take part too since 1.16, their charge on one atom as a whole unit, so a proton does not hop from H₃O⁺ to the next water. Its limits for now: a molecule that touches a metal or an ion's stored bond, or holds an atom charged twice, goes back to stored bonds; crowded molecules such as SF₆ hold together since 1.16 (two atoms bonded to one centre much more strongly than to each other don't bond to each other, as a stored molecule's 1-3 pairs don't). Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath. Since 1.20 an atom's electrons are a continuous count that moves within its molecule, so a charge can follow a proton: a proton passing from H₃O⁺ to a neighbouring water takes the +1 with it, and a proton shared between two waters (as in the Zundel ion) holds the charge half on each. Every pair is worked out every step, and steps are 2.6 times shorter.

In the flat game the angles hold each bond's gap to its neighbours round the atom. Costs ×8 (liquid water) to ×15 (the molecule gallery), on the WebAssembly pass.

In the 3-D lab (since 1.25) the angles are the stored bonds' 3-D ones (each pair of bonds at its VSEPR angle, the bipyramid's and octahedron's roles), double and aromatic bonds resist twisting as their orders grow, and single bonds twist against their measured barriers (ethane 0.126 eV, butane, methanol, methylamine, hydrogen peroxide within a few meV of the measured), each weighted by the orders. Its pass runs in WebAssembly in 3-D too since 1.26 (it ran in JavaScript in 1.25: ×18–23): ×7–14 (liquid water ×7–8, the molecules in space ×8, a box of organic molecules ×14; measured in 3-D).

Quantum (GFN1-xTB, since 1.22): the atoms' energy and forces come from their electrons, worked out at every step by GFN1-xTB (Grimme, Bannwarth and Shushkov, 2017), a tight-binding quantum chemistry: a minimal basis of atomic orbitals, the electrons shared out over molecular orbitals, the charges made self-consistent, with the D3 dispersion it was made for. It matches the reference program (tblite) to 10⁻¹¹ eV and its forces to 10⁻¹⁰ eV/Å. Bonds, charges and barriers all come from the electrons: a hydronium in water passes its proton on (the oxygens close in to 2.4–2.6 Å, a shared proton rattles between them and moves on to new waters within a picosecond), which the force fields cannot do. Every atom's electrons are worked out together, so it is for small systems: up to 40 atoms of any element up to radon, and up to 160 atomic orbitals (a light atom has 1–4, an atom from silicon on 9; with quantum nuclei, fewer: each bead is a calculation of its own); more, and none take part; the rest of the world meets them through its usual pairs and their charges. Bonds are drawn from the electrons (Mayer bond orders). Leaving it, molecules get stored bonds and whole charges where the electrons put them. Costs below.

In the flat game the atoms stay in the plane; their orbitals are three-dimensional.

In the 3-D lab the atoms move in all three directions, as GFN1-xTB has them (hexazine's flat ring puckers there); a hydronium and five waters take 5.7 ms a step (the flat game 7.7).

Quantum method

Quantum method (since 1.26): GFN1-xTB, as since 1.22, or GFN2-xTB (Bannwarth, Ehlert and Grimme, 2019), its successor: the same kind of calculation with one orbital on hydrogen, the electrons' pull worked out to atomic dipoles and quadrupoles, and the D4 dispersion, whose strength follows each atom's charge. It matches the reference program (tblite) to 10⁻¹¹ eV and its forces to 10⁻¹⁰ eV/Å; in the game the forces are the energy's slopes to 10⁻⁶ eV/Å and an insulated hydronium with five waters keeps its energy to 10⁻⁴ eV over 200 steps, at about the same cost as GFN1. Measured against reference values (1.26): bond lengths alike (0.011 and 0.012 Å from the measured on 24 bonds); water's dimer alike (0.21 and 0.22 eV, measured 0.22); GFN2 closer for breaking molecules into atoms (both far too strong: 4.6 eV off on average, GFN1 6.6), but further off for barriers: H + H₂ (measured 0.42 eV) GFN1 0.15, while GFN2 binds H₃ by 0.40 eV; HCN to HNC (2.09 eV) GFN1 2.52, GFN2 3.18; a proton between two waters 2.7 Å apart (0.10 eV by g-xTB) GFN1 0.08, GFN2 0.04; and hexazine, which really falls apart into three N₂ releasing several eV: GFN1 holds it with a 0.54 eV barrier and releases 1.7 eV, GFN2 puts the ring 0.8 eV (flat) to 1.7 eV (puckered) below three N₂. So GFN1 stays the normal choice.

Spins

Spins (since 1.26): with Polarized, the electrons of each spin get orbitals of their own, as in the published spin-polarized GFN-xTB (Neugebauer, Bädorf, Ehlert, Hansen and Grimme, 2023), ported from the reference program (matched to 10⁻¹¹ eV and its forces to 10⁻⁶ eV/Å on 56 radicals, atoms and stretched bonds). Unpaired electrons line up, so a free atom or a radical gets the energy of its spin (nitrogen 3.1 eV, oxygen 1.5, hydrogen 1.0), oxygen's O₂ is the triplet it really is, and a bond pulled apart ends as two radicals instead of an unsettled half-and-half (H₂ given 8 eV: every step settles, against unsettled steps with eV jumps when paired). Each cluster finds its own spins (one Fermi level for both): the electrons are checked at every step for a lower spin arrangement, and when one appears the switch's energy goes to the heat bath. Measured against reference values (1.26): breaking 23 molecules into atoms 2.7 eV off on average with GFN1-xTB (paired 6.6), 1.6 with GFN2-xTB (paired 4.6); H + H₂ (measured 0.42 eV) GFN1 0.58 (paired 0.15), GFN2 0.03 (paired −0.40: H₃ bound); ozone losing an O (1.15 eV) GFN1 5.5 (7.6), GFN2 4.7 (6.9). Molecules without unpaired electrons (water, its dimer, the shared proton, HCN, hexazine) are unchanged. It costs ×1.2–1.6 where every electron is paired, ×1.6–2.3 among radicals.

Bond orders for metals

Metals

Normally metal atoms hold each other by pairs: a Morse bond between each two neighbours, its depth set so that a metal melts near its real melting point in the flat world. With Many-body, a metal atom's bonding comes from how many neighbours it has (the second-moment tight-binding model of Finnis and Sinclair and of Cleri and Rosato, the square-root case of the embedded-atom method): each atom holds the square root of the sum of its neighbours' hoppings, less a repulsion between the ion cores. One bond is then worth more than its share of six or twelve: a lone pair of atoms is short and strong, an atom at an edge or surface holds harder to each neighbour it keeps, and pulling an atom out of a crystal (a vacancy) costs about a third of the cohesive energy rather than all of it. Each metal is fitted to its measured cohesive energy, nearest-neighbour distance, bulk modulus and vacancy energy on a close-packed crystal, and the 3-D lab gives those values exactly (copper: 3.49 eV an atom, 2.56 Å, 137 GPa, 1.28 eV). Alloys mix the two metals' constants: silver dissolves in copper at a cost of 0.30 eV (measured about 0.39); strongly bound pairs have constants of their own (1.26), fitted to a measured compound (NiAl, NiTi, FeAl, CuZn, Cu₃Au, TiAl), so an aluminium atom in nickel holds 1.7 eV more than in its own metal and Ni₃Al and CuAu come out within 3% of their measured heats of formation, and Cu₃Au is lower ordered than at random. Costs ×1–2 where there are metal atoms (one more pass over them each step), nothing elsewhere. Ions are not part of it: an atom that loses or gains an electron leaves the metal. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath. Small clusters are bound too tightly, as in every model of this kind: a copper pair holds 2.9 eV (measured 2.0). Oxides and hydrides (1.26): an O or H atom with a free valence bonds to the metal through the same square-root form, with constants of its own for each metal fitted to a measured compound (its heat of formation at its measured spacing and stiffness: Cu₂O, Ag₂O, Al₂O₃, Cr₂O₃, FeO, NiO, MgO, TiO₂, ZnO, Na₂O, NaH, LiH, MgH₂, TiH₂, PdH and more; other metals take their group's), so a metal atom's bonds to its neighbours weaken as it oxidizes. O₂, H₂ and water meeting the metal may split on it, the energy measured as for any reaction. An ion near the metal feels the pull of its image charge. Ions of the metal still leave it, and its bonds to O and H are drawn as no lines. Directional bonding (1.26): in the body-centred metals (tungsten, molybdenum, chromium, vanadium, niobium, tantalum, iron, and sodium) how the neighbours are arranged around an atom counts too, as the lobes of their d orbitals make it (angular terms in the band energy, as in the modified embedded-atom method), fitted to each one’s body-centred cubic crystal (cohesive energy and spacing exactly, stiffness, vacancy energy, and how far the close-packed fcc and hcp lie above it), so they crystallize body-centred cubic, as they really do. The other metals keep the close-packed fit.

In the flat world a sheet has six neighbours, not twelve, so copper holds 2.73 eV an atom (with pairs 0.78) at 2.45 Å, and flat metals melt too high: a flat patch of 241 atoms melts at about 1,000 K for aluminium (real 933 K), 2,000 K for copper (1,358 K) and 5,500 K for tungsten (3,695 K). With directional bonding (1.26) tungsten, molybdenum and chromium sheets turn rectangular, the pattern of a body-centred crystal’s densest face (0.1–0.2 eV an atom below the triangular sheet), sodium sheets square, and iron, vanadium, niobium and tantalum stay nearly triangular.

In the 3-D lab a crystal has its twelve neighbours, as fitted, and the measured values above come out exactly; a ball of 739 copper atoms melts from about 1,300 K (a small ball melts below the bulk's 1,358 K). Costs in 3-D: an aluminium ball of 959 atoms ×1.8, a copper ball ×0.8–1.0 (measured, 1.25). The body-centred metals (1.26) have their measured cohesive energy and spacing on the body-centred cubic crystal, the close-packed ones above it (tungsten fcc +0.47 eV an atom, hcp +0.57; iron +0.09, +0.11; measured about 0.48 and 0.1), their measured C₄₄ and bulk modulus within 3% (sodium 6%) and vacancy energies within 7%, while C₁₁ − C₁₂ comes out 20–60% low; a tungsten ball started fcc turns body-centred within a picosecond at 600 K. Their angles cost ×1.04 a step in a tungsten crystal of 1,024 atoms.

Directional forces

Lone pairs

Normally each atom's partial charge sits at its nucleus, so a hydrogen bond pulls an oxygen's or nitrogen's partner toward its centre from any side. With Lone pairs (since 1.26, the 3-D lab only), an oxygen with two single bonds (water, alcohols, ethers) carries its partial charge on two lone pairs 0.7 Å out at the tetrahedral directions, and a nitrogen with three single bonds (ammonia, amines) on one along its pyramid's axis, as the TIP5P water model does: a hydrogen bond then forms where a lone pair points, and water's neighbours sit at the corners of a tetrahedron. Between molecules only (a molecule's own atoms keep their point charges), fading back to point charges over the last ångström of the force reach. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath. Measured in the 3-D lab (1.26): the water dimer's acceptor turns its lone pair to the donor, tilted 42° (point charges 17°; measured about 57°, TIP5P 51°), its oxygens 2.77 Å apart, bound 0.42 eV (point charges 0.21; measured 0.22: a pair model made for the liquid binds a lone pair of molecules harder, as TIP5P's 0.29 does); with the 9 Å force reach liquid water has 4.5 neighbours inside its first minimum (measured 4.3–4.7; point charges 6.0), its first peak at 2.78 Å (measured 2.80) and ice keeps its crystal at 250 K (point charges: its second neighbours crushed from 4.5 to 4.1 Å). Still off: liquid water comes out about 17% too dense, as with point charges (14%), and with the usual 6 Å reach the cutoff blurs the network. Costs ×2–2.6 in liquid water.

Halogen σ-holes

A chlorine, bromine or iodine bonded to carbon (or to another halogen) is negative around its belt but positive on the far tip of its bond, where its bonding electron is pulled away: the σ-hole. A lone pair can bind there, straight along the bond (a halogen bond, C–X···N at 180°). With σ-holes on (since 1.26, both modes), each such halogen carries a small positive point beyond it on the bond's axis (Cl 0.10 e at 1.6 Å, Br 0.085 e at 1.9 Å, I 0.08 e at 2.2 Å, the atom as much more negative), as force fields' "extra point" models do. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath. Measured (1.26, kcal/mol): CH₃Br···NH₃ 1.9 at 3.24 Å and 179° (without: 1.4, the ammonia off the axis at 4.0 Å; reference about 1.7–2.0), CH₃I···NH₃ 2.9 at 3.41 Å (about 2.6–3.2). Molecules whose carbon is very positive here (CF₃X: the game's C–F charges are about twice the real ones) bind two or three times too hard. Costs ×1–2.7 where there are such halogens, nothing elsewhere.

π-stacking

An aromatic ring's π electrons lie above and below it, so a ring is negative on its faces and positive round its rim, and two rings stack offset or meet edge to face rather than face to face. With π clouds on (since 1.26, the 3-D lab only), every aromatic carbon carries 0.07 e on two points 0.7 Å above and below the ring (the carbon as much more positive), and ring atoms of two aromatic molecules meet with a dispersion fitted to the benzene dimer instead of the game's general one, which in space binds rings about three times too hard. Changing it books the energy difference to the heat bath. Measured in the 3-D lab (1.26, kcal/mol, rigid rings): the benzene dimer face to face −1.95 at 3.8 Å, edge to face −2.84 at 5.0 Å, offset −2.77 at 3.5 Å (CCSD(T): about −1.7 to −1.8 at 3.9, −2.6 to −2.8 at 5.0, −2.6 to −2.8 at 3.5–3.6 Å with a 1.6–1.8 Å offset; here the offset comes out 2.6 Å); without it −8.6, −4.5 and −8.4. Costs up to ×10 in a box of benzene, nothing without aromatic rings.

Electrons as waves

Each free electron and positron becomes a wave packet with a size and a spin (the electron force field of Su and Goddard, eFF): squeezing a packet costs kinetic energy (3ħ²/2mₑs², 11.4 eV at 1 Å), two packets meet as spread-out charges, and two electrons whose waves overlap push apart, strongly with the same spin and weakly with opposite ones (the Pauli principle). So free electrons crowd less, and a dense electron gas holds itself up. The packets' sizes move as coordinates of their own, and their energy is part of the books. With ions and atoms the free electrons still meet as points, so captures, scattering and ionization stay as calibrated; an atom's own electrons are not particles here and stay as they are. A free packet spreads to 2 Å at most (beyond, a spring holds it, as eFF's users bound it). A packet that appears or goes away hands its energy to its own motion, or, when that can't take it, to the heat bath. Measured: the forces are the energy's slopes to 10⁻⁶ eV/Å.

Drawn as a faint disc. Costs ×6.5 in liquid water with 40 free electrons (their own steps 13 times shorter, for the packets' breathing, which takes a few hundredths of a femtosecond), nothing where there are none; that water keeps its energy to a few eV a picosecond (about as without).

In the 3-D lab the packets meet in all three directions, as eFF is made, and are drawn as points (their size is not drawn yet). Costs in 3-D: 40 free electrons among neon atoms ×2.2 (the flat game ×2.4), six Na⁺ among twelve ×3.2; in ionized liquid water the electrons are caught again within a few dozen steps in 3-D, so it costs little there (measured, 1.25).

A slow negative muon, pion or kaon (and in the flat game an antiproton) is caught by an atom into an orbit, as in nature: it slows by ionizing until it moves no faster than the atom's outer electrons (tens of eV), and is caught by an atom it is passing, each with a chance in proportion to its charge Z (Fermi and Teller). It takes the place of an electron, which flies off, and falls down the orbits from n ≈ 14 (a muon; a pion 16, a kaon or antiproton 30–43): the upper part of that cascade sets Auger electrons free, the lower part gives off the exotic atom's X-ray lines, which screens and detectors see (muonic carbon 2p → 1s 75 keV, muonic lead 5.90 MeV, μp 1.9 keV), all in about 10⁻¹³ s, so at once here. Then a muon decays (its electron with the Michel spectrum) or is captured by the nucleus, at the rates measured for each element (2.0 µs in carbon, 864 ns in aluminium, 75 ns in lead); a pion is absorbed by the nucleus, which breaks up into a star of neutrons, protons and light nuclei (in hydrogen π⁻p → π⁰n or γn, 3 to 2); a kaon makes a Λ or Σ and pions; an antiproton annihilates (antiprotons and protonium, p̄p, are in the flat game only). A muonic atom is an atom of the box (Select, Erase, Inspect, copy, saves and undo take it) whose electrons see one charge less: muonic carbon bonds with boron's valence, bond energies and size. Muonic hydrogen is neutral and 200 times smaller than an atom: it passes through atoms, and its muon moves over to a heavier atom beside it (to oxygen at 8.5×10¹⁰ s⁻¹ in liquid-hydrogen density). Off: as before (a muon caught at 2 keV, pions, kaons and antiprotons absorbed at once, the electrons of a muonic atom seeing the whole nucleus). Since 1.27 a stopped K⁻ makes Σπ twice as often as Λπ, the Σ often turning into a Λ on a second nucleon, and hydrogen bound in water, methane and other hydrocarbons catches the particle at the measured, far smaller share than its Z gives.

What carries over to the chemistry of a muonic atom: its element's table entries (valence, bond energies and lengths, size, electronegativity, ionization energies, charge). What does not: rules written for one element by its nuclear charge (a muonic helium is hydrogen-like, but makes no hydrogen bonds), the continuous bond orders and quantum bonds (they take the nucleus's Z), and the cascade's holes filling from the neighbours (the atom keeps at most +2). The strong interaction's shift of the last pionic or kaonic line is left out.

In the 3-D lab the same for muons, pions and kaons, in all three directions; antiprotons are not in 3-D (antimatter is flat-only).

A slow positron takes an electron and becomes positronium (Ps), an electron and a positron going round each other: bound by 6.8 eV (half hydrogen's binding), twice hydrogen's size, a thousandth of its mass. From an atom or molecule it forms in the Ore gap, when the positron's energy lies between the electron's binding less 6.8 eV and the binding (hydrogen 6.8–13.6 eV; at a negative ion such as Cl⁻ at any energy); below that it can't, above it the positron annihilates as before. From a free electron (the ones its own track set free) it forms when the two meet slowly, a photon taking the difference. One positronium in four is para-Ps (the spins opposite): it lives 125 ps and gives two γ rays of 511 keV back to back. Three in four are ortho-Ps (the spins parallel): in vacuum it lives 142 ns, which is forever at this timescale, and gives three γ rays in a plane sharing 1,022 keV (each anywhere up to 511 keV, the Ore–Powell spectrum); in matter its positron meets an electron of the molecules round it much sooner (pick-off, two γ rays: 1.8 ns in water, measured), at a rate set here by the electron density round it; O₂'s unpaired electrons turn ortho into para. Inspect shows a positronium's state, binding and expected life where it is. Positronium is an unstable particle in flight (Select, Erase, saves and undo take it). What it doesn't do here: it passes through atoms (in nature it pushes them back into a bubble about 3 Å across, which the pick-off rate stands for), keeps the energy it was made with, and makes no compounds (PsH). Off: as before (a positron touching an electron annihilates).

Muonium (a positive muon with an electron, made when a slow μ⁺ takes one) is chemically a light hydrogen atom: 0.114 u, a ninth of hydrogen's mass, bound by 13.54 eV (hydrogen 13.60: the reduced mass), bonding by hydrogen's rules (MuH, MuCl, MuOH), so its isotope effects are large: Mu–Cl vibrates 2.9 times faster than H–Cl, and with Quantum nuclei its zero-point energy is about three times hydrogen's. Its muon decays in 2.2 µs as before. The muon's spin (μSR): a muon fired or born from a pion decay has its spin against its flight, one placed at rest across the field. In a magnetic field the spin precesses: 135.5 MHz a tesla for a bare muon or one in a molecule; muonium with its electron along the field turns at 1.39 MHz a gauss in low fields (the triplet; Breit–Rabi above about 0.1 T), the other half at about the hyperfine frequency (4,463 MHz), which averages out. The decay positron comes out preferentially along the spin (on average 1 + cos θ / 3, more so for the fastest), so a ring of detectors round a muon sample in a field counts the precession. Inspect shows the spin and its frequency; the particle view draws it as a small arrow. Not modelled: muonated radicals' own hyperfine frequencies (they turn as a bare muon here), spin relaxation, chemical shifts. Off: as before (no spins, the decay positron isotropic).

A fast charged particle crossing matter loses its energy collision by collision, as in nature: mostly in small knocks, now and then in a hard one that sends an electron flying with up to the most a collision can give (a δ-ray, which ionizes in turn; 2.7 keV from a 5 MeV α, half its energy from an electron), so the loss over a thin layer has Landau's long tail instead of a fixed amount. On average it is Bethe's formula with each element's measured excitation energy: fast particles lose more than they did before (the relativistic rise the game's form lacked: in the flat game's water a 5 MeV α 1.6 times, its measured 89 keV a micrometre; slow ions as before). Electrons and positrons give off bremsstrahlung (photons with a 1/k spectrum) at the rate set by each material's radiation length (lead 5.6 mm, water 36 cm), so it overtakes the collisions above the critical energy (water 79 MeV, lead 8 MeV). γ rays above 1.022 MeV make an electron and a positron near a nucleus (or two electrons and a positron in an electron's field) at the measured cross sections, against Compton scattering at the Klein–Nishina rate: the pair flies forward and shares the energy unequally. In water or glass a particle faster than light there (c/n) gives off Cherenkov light on a cone: 41° in water, 47° in silica glass, 334 photons a centimetre in 300–600 nm, which screens see. What a nanometre box can't make real: a radiation length is centimetres, so at nature's rates a fast electron almost never radiates in the box, Cherenkov light is one photon in thousands of crossings, a shower (centimetres long) can't develop, and a minimum-ionizing particle mostly loses far less than its average (the rare hard collisions carry most of it); what is made leaves the box. γ rays meet atoms as often as the game's light always has (about 10⁷ times nature's). Off: as before (a smooth loss, bremsstrahlung by a rule of thumb, pairs back to back sharing evenly). Experimental: off again each time the game starts.

Matter depth

With Charged particles in matter on: speeds up its rare processes, bremsstrahlung and Cherenkov light, by the factor chosen, as if the box were that much deeper, so that in a box nanometres across fast electrons radiate, their γ rays make pairs (γ rays meet atoms at the game's high rate already) and a shower can start, and Cherenkov cones show. It speeds up only those rare processes: the collisions, δ-rays and energy loss stay at nature's rates, so a scaled shower is not nature's in its details. Its critical energy (where radiating overtakes the collisions: water 79 MeV, lead 8 MeV) comes out lower by about the same factor, and the radiation length shorter by it. ×1 is nature. Off (or Charged particles in matter off): nothing changes. Experimental: back to ×1 each time the game starts.

A resonance lives about 10⁻²³ s, so its mass is not sharp: by the uncertainty principle it spreads over its width Γ = ħ/τ. With this on, each resonance made (ρ, ω, φ, K*, Δ, J/ψ, Υ: in a collision, placed, fired, or among a decay's products) gets its own mass, drawn from the relativistic Breit–Wigner of its measured width, cut at the lightest pair it can decay into: a ρ⁰ (775 MeV, Γ 149 MeV) anywhere from 280 MeV up, a Δ (1,232 MeV, Γ 117 MeV) from 1,078 MeV. It decays from that mass, and the collision that made it pays for it exactly. And a hadron collision (protons and neutrons, pions or antiprotons on nucleons, the collider's hadron beams) above the charm threshold makes a charm–anticharm pair at the share of collisions measured at its energy (σ(cc̄)/σ_inel: 1 in 700 at 40 GeV, 1 in 70 at 200 GeV, 1 in 9 at 7 TeV), now and then bottom (1 in 260 at 7 TeV), each quark as a D⁰, D⁺, D⁺s or Λc⁺ (B⁺, B⁰, B⁰s or Λb⁰) at the measured fractions, a few in a thousand of them a J/ψ or an Υ. The collider's list shows them. A D⁰ lives 0.41 ps and a B 1.5 ps: at their speed they fly micrometres to millimetres before they decay, far out of a box of nanometres, so most leave it unless decay is sped up. Since 1.27 nucleus–nucleus collisions make charm and bottom by their nucleon–nucleon collisions, and e⁺e⁻ near the Z feels the beams' own radiation (the Z peak 30.5 nb, as measured, not 41.5) and makes W pairs with the W⁻ forward along the electron.

Three things nuclei do in nature. Spin: each nucleus has its measured spin and magnetic moment (¹H ½ and 2.793 μN, ²H 1, ¹³C ½, ¹⁷O 5/2, ¹⁹F ½, ³¹P ½ …; nuclei with even numbers of protons and neutrons none), shown in Inspect; in a magnetic field the spin precesses at the Larmor frequency (42.58 MHz per tesla for ¹H, 10.71 for ¹³C), and Inspect's NMR pulse tips the spins of that kind of nucleus over and reads the frequency of their signal. γ lines: a nucleus left excited by a decay, a neutron capture or a reaction gives off its measured lines instead of one lump: ⁶⁰Co β⁻ → ⁶⁰Ni 1,173 + 1,332 keV, ¹³⁷Cs → ¹³⁷ᵐBa (it waits 2.6 min) → 662 keV, capture on hydrogen 2,224 keV, on ¹⁴N up to 10.83 MeV, on ¹¹³Cd the 558 keV line, ¹⁰B(n,α) the 478 keV line of ⁷Li; where a line converts often, an atomic electron leaves in its place; isomers wait their half-lives (⁹⁹ᵐTc 6 h). Elsewhere, as before. Neutrons: each nuclide's measured thermal cross sections for capture, scattering and fission (²³⁵U fission 585 b, ¹¹³Cd 20,600 b, ¹⁰B 3,840 b, ¹⁵⁷Gd 254,000 b, ¹H 0.33 b), 1/v below about 1 eV, the big resonances (²³⁸U at 6.67 eV), and the neutron's spin ½ (−1.913 μN). The game's nuclei are drawn and hit far larger than they are, so in the normal view a neutron meets every nucleus about as often, and the cross sections decide what a meeting does; with Ultra-realistic on, nuclei are hit at their measured cross sections, so ¹⁰B and cadmium take neutrons thousands of times more readily than hydrogen, as measured.

Light is a wave whose electric field swings across its path, along its polarization. With this on, every photon carries one: in the flat game in the plane, across it (out of the screen), or between. The Light tool and lasers shine polarized light (choose in the plane, across it, or unpolarized; in 3-D vertical or horizontal); atoms, lamps, hot walls and nuclei give unpolarized light; the two 511 keV γ rays of an electron and a positron annihilating (also para-positronium's) are polarized at right angles to each other. Compton scattering of γ rays follows the Klein–Nishina formula, with the polarization: at low energy as much forward as back (Thomson, 1 + cos²θ), at MeV energies mostly forward; the scattered photon keeps E′ = E/(1 + (E/mₑc²)(1 − cos θ)), leaves least along the polarization (at low energy none at 90° in its direction), and takes a new polarization. Placed objects in the flat game: mirrors and beam splitters keep the polarization; a prism's faces reflect a few percent of the light by Fresnel's laws, and at Brewster's angle (58° for its flint glass) only light polarized across the plane; the Objects tool has a polarizer (Malus's law: cos² of the angle between the light's polarization and its axis passes). Inspect shows each photon's polarization. What it can't make real: circular and elliptical polarization (a total reflection's phase shifts), and the annihilation pair's quantum entanglement: each pair here is a definite perpendicular pair, which gives the single photons the right statistics but a weaker correlation between the two (the ratio of their Compton scatterings at right angles to parallel at 82°: 1.63 here, 2.85 in quantum mechanics). With Charged particles in matter on, Compton's odds also follow the total Klein–Nishina cross section (it falls with energy: 0.43 of Thomson's at 511 keV), and Cherenkov light comes out polarized in the plane of the track and the photon. Off: as before (no polarization, Compton angles drawn evenly in cos θ). Since 1.27 the two photons of π⁰ → γγ and η → γγ are polarized at right angles too, and ⁶⁰Co's two γ rays (1,173 and 1,332 keV, with Nuclear spin, γ lines on) leave with their measured angular correlation, 1.17 times as likely back to back as at right angles.

Light that cannot ionize (infrared, visible, near ultraviolet) is scattered by molecules and atoms: its field drives the electrons to swing, and they send it on in a new direction with the same colour (since 1.27). The rate goes as 1/λ⁴, so blue light (400 nm) scatters 9.4 times as often as red (700 nm), the reason the sky is blue and sunsets red; a molecule scatters as the square of its polarizability (benzene about 35 times as much as nitrogen, argon about as much). The light goes forward and back twice as often as sideways (1 + cos²θ), and what is scattered at right angles comes out polarized (with Photon polarization on, it carries it). Each molecule uses its polarizability by direction, so a long or flat one (CO₂, benzene) depolarizes the light a little. In a box a few nanometres across the real rate is almost nothing (a photon crosses about 80 km of air before it scatters once), so it is shown made visible: about 1 photon in 100 is scattered by each nitrogen molecule it passes at 450 nm (in the flat game's liquid water, 7% of blue photons and 0.5% of red ones crossing the box), the ratios between colours and molecules the true ones. With ultra-realistic mode on, the true rate: about 10⁻¹⁰ a molecule passed. The photon keeps its energy, so nothing else changes.

What it does, and does not

  • A two-dimensional world (the 3-D lab: a cube) with real constants: atoms move by classical mechanics under forces fitted to real chemistry (bonds, angles, charges); free electrons and nuclei are particles of their own; nuclear reactions, decays and particle physics come from measured data. Energy is kept in the books, and checked for every experiment.
  • Simplified: the world is flat (but in the 3-D lab); the electrons inside an atom are not separate particles (an atom carries its electrons as a cloud with measured energies); light travels as rays; very fast particles fly straight between collisions instead of being stepped.
  • Known limits: water stripped of many electrons (oxygen ions of charge +4 and +5 among free electrons) gains about 100–170 eV over a run; worlds of many thousands of atoms run slower than 1× on slower computers (Settings → Benchmark measures yours).
